# Nine Nights - The Story of Shakti: the plan

This file is the approved plan for all nine nights (16 October 2026): each night's Devi, main idea, Harry Potter scenes, strength of the comparison and cautions. The full lesson and read-aloud script for each night are in `docs/nights/night-N.md`. Components must not contain lesson text; it is copied from those files into `src/content/`.

Chapter numbers follow the UK editions. Book abbreviations: PS = Philosopher's Stone, CoS = Chamber of Secrets, PoA = Prisoner of Azkaban, GoF = Goblet of Fire, OotP = Order of the Phoenix, HBP = Half-Blood Prince, DH = Deathly Hallows.


Five nights rest on a strong match at the level of decisions, three are partial or partial to strong, and Night 4 (Kushmanda) is honestly speculative.

## Who this is for

- **One parent and one pre-teen (about 10–12), together, every night.** Nothing assumes a particular family.
- **The child has read all seven books and seen all eight films.** Any scene in the series can be used, including the ending.
- **Spoiler notice:** the opening screen and Night 1 say plainly that the series discusses the whole of Harry Potter, including major twists and deaths. Families who haven't finished it may want to wait.
- **Intensity still matters.** Scenes involving death, torture or deep grief are tagged *intense* and carry a note for the parent.
- **Three layers of questions:** for the child; "go deeper" for a child ready to stretch; one question for parent and child together.
- **The Marvel version** is not published. This plan follows the original brief and does not need to avoid any of its themes.

## Overall approach

Each night starts with the Devi's story, finds the *decision* inside it, and only then asks where a Harry Potter character faces a similar decision. If no honest match exists, the night says so.

**How a night is built (about 25–30 minutes)**

1. The traditional story, told plainly, with its source named (5–7 min).
2. The night's theme: one word, a one-line idea children can remember, and a short reading (see "The nine themes" below).
3. Two Harry Potter scenes: a primary one and one that complements or contrasts. Each gives book, chapter and film, says what is similar and what is different, and rates the strength of the link.
4. A present-day dilemma with several perspectives, not a verdict.
5. Questions for the child, "go deeper" questions, and one for parent and child together, plus one 5–15 minute thinking activity.
6. A short note for the parent, with sources and confidence.

**Short evening (about 15 minutes):** a switch in the app gives a shorter night: the opening, the story without its choices, the theme, the first Harry Potter scene with its Think it through (two ladder questions), and Reflect. Each night file says exactly what its short evening keeps. (This replaces the older "short path".)

**Labels on every claim**

- **Tradition:** the account in a named text or regional tradition (e.g. Devi Mahatmya ch. 2, Shiva Purana, Bhagavata Purana, the Tamil Kanda Puranam).
- **Interpretation:** a traditional devotional or philosophical reading (e.g. the yogic chakra mapping of the nine forms).
- **Contemporary:** a modern reading. Reading the nine nights as a personal-development journey belongs here: it is a useful modern frame, not an ancient teaching.

**Rules for the comparisons**

- Compare choices, dilemmas and consequences, never powers, looks or weapons.
- Never present a Harry Potter character as "the" Devi or a version of her. A Devi is a sacred figure worshipped today; Harry Potter characters are fictional. Magic in the books is a learned and inherited skill, while Shakti is understood as divine power and the ground of existence.
- Every comparison states the differences as clearly as the similarities, and is rated **strong**, **partial** or **speculative**.
- Harry Potter's death-and-return imagery (the forest walk, King's Cross, "master of death") is offered as *our* comparison, never as hidden theology. Never suggest that J.K. Rowling or the filmmakers intended a parallel.
- Paraphrase every quote. Any direct quotation stays well under 15 words, and they are rare.

**The opening splash (new wording, 18 October 2026, for Supriya to review):** the app's name small at the top, then one large question, the four splash questions (from the Recurring themes table), a short introduction, a note on the two kinds of story, "Begin the journey", and the one-sentence disclaimer.

- **The large question:** What is power for?
- **Introduction:** Over nine nights we meet nine forms of the Devi and hear their traditional stories. Then we look at the same questions through scenes from the Harry Potter books and films.
- **Note:** These are different kinds of story. The Devi is sacred in a living tradition; Harry Potter is fiction. We compare the choices people make, never the Devi with a character.

**Disclaimer (shown with "How we compare"; the full disclaimer, with the splash screen's one-sentence version, is in `docs/DISCLAIMER.md`; draft wording, 17 October 2026, for Supriya to review):** The Harry Potter books and films were not written with Navaratri or the Devi in mind. The links we draw between them are our own interpretation.

**Spoilers and intensity**

- Spoilers are allowed: any scene from the seven books and eight films can be used. A spoiler notice opens the series.
- Each scene is tagged *gentle*, *some peril* or *intense*. Intense scenes (deaths, torture, deep grief) come with a short note for the parent.
- Note any film difference that changes the choice being discussed.
- **Sources for the nine forms:** the nine names come from the Devi Kavacham, a hymn recited with the Devi Mahatmya and traditionally attributed to the Varaha Purana (to check against a printed edition). Most individual stories come from Puranas or devotional tradition, and each night says which.

## The nine themes (agreed 17 October 2026)

Each night has one **theme**: a single word, a one-line idea children can remember, and a longer description. The story ends on the theme, and the night ends on it again. The descriptions are readings, so the app labels them **interpretation**.

### Night 1 · Shailaputri · Rootedness
What you are rooted in can survive what you lose.

The first form of the Devi is named for a relationship, not for something she did: she is the daughter of the mountain. The festival starts with where you stand before it asks what you’ll do. Sati loses her body, her family and her home. What survives is her devotion, which returns whole in Parvati, rooted in a new place. Devotees often link Shailaputri with the mountain’s steadiness. Our reading is that your roots are less about where you live or who you came from, and more about what you’re devoted to. That’s what carries you through change.

### Night 2 · Brahmacharini · Resolve
What you practise, day after day, changes who you are.

Her name means “one who practises”. Parvati first tried the quick way, with Kama’s arrow and her own beauty, and it went up in flames. Only then did she choose the slow path of tapas, season after season. Her final test isn’t about how much she can endure. Shiva, in disguise, mocks her choice to see whether she still knows why she’s doing it. In this story, resolve is less about toughness than about a reason that survives being laughed at. The practice wins Shiva, but first it changes her.

### Night 3 · Chandraghanta · Readiness
Strength can protect just by being ready.

Her emblem is a bell, and in the Devi Mahatmya the bell protects by its sound alone: the ringing that overwhelms demons reassures the gods. She is pictured with a calm face and weapons in every hand. She isn’t calm first and fierce later. She is both at once, fierce towards harm and serene towards the frightened. Calling her simply “a warrior” misses this. Her strength is often a signal that stops a fight before it has to happen. Readiness, held calmly, is itself a kind of protection.

### Night 4 · Kushmanda · Creation
What you make carries something of why you made it.

In her story the universe isn’t built by force or labour. It begins with a smile in the dark, and a small warm egg of light. Old Indian philosophy asks why anyone would create at all, and one answer is lila, play: creation out of delight, not need. And Kushmanda doesn’t leave what she made. She lives at the core of the sun, keeping it warm. Our reading is that why you make something shows up in what it becomes, and that making something starts a relationship with it rather than finishing a task.

### Night 5 · Skandamata · Nurture
True care helps someone grow strong enough to go their own way.

Skanda’s story is full of carers: a fire god, a river, six star-mothers, and Parvati. Each carries him for a while; none of them keeps him. Parvati gathers him close, and then, when the gods need him, she sends him out. In Tamil tradition she arms him with the vel, a spear holding her own power. She’s pictured with the child on her lap, facing outward towards the world. Nurture here means both holding close and getting ready to let go. Good care prepares someone to need you less.

### Night 6 · Katyayani · Courage
When something is wrong, stand up to it, and stand together.

Mahisha had driven the gods out of heaven, and each of them, alone, had lost. Their shared anger at the injustice blazed out as light and gathered into one form: the Devi. The Devi Mahatmya says she is eternal and simply appears when she is needed. Each god then gave her his own weapon, trusting her with what he couldn’t win alone. In a popular telling (not in the Devi Mahatmya), Mahisha had asked that only a woman could kill him, because he thought no woman could. In this story, courage isn’t the absence of fear. It’s refusing to accept a wrong, and it’s strongest when it’s shared. Often it comes from exactly where the arrogant weren’t looking.

### Night 7 · Kalaratri · Fearlessness
Face the dark without becoming part of it.

Kalaratri is the darkest form: unbound hair, three blazing eyes, fire on her breath. Yet she’s called Shubhankari, “the one who does good”, and her raised hand says “do not fear”. Her fierceness is aimed at what harms, not at the people she protects. In the Raktabija story, every blow made the demon multiply. Kali didn’t strike harder. She caught each drop before it reached the ground. Fearlessness, in this reading, isn’t fighting the dark louder. It’s facing it steadily, refusing to feed it, and not letting it change you.

### Night 8 · Mahagauri · Renewal
After a hard time, you can begin again, clearer than before.

In a common devotional telling, Parvati’s long tapas left her covered in the dust and darkness of years outdoors. When Shiva accepted her, he washed her with the waters of the Ganga, and she shone radiant: Mahagauri, “the greatly bright one”. She’s pictured calm, dressed in white, riding a bull, with hands of fearlessness and blessing. Renewal here doesn’t mean pretending the hard time never happened. The tapas made her who she is. What washes away is the weight; what stays is what you learned. She’s linked with calm, forgiveness and fresh starts.

**For the parent:** some tellings frame this as dark skin becoming fair, and in some Shiva teases her about her colour. Tell it as washing away the dust of a hard journey, and name the colour version openly with your child rather than repeating it uncritically.

### Night 9 · Siddhidatri · Wholeness
You are whole when you know you are part of the whole.

Siddhidatri is the giver of siddhis, perfected powers, and many images show gods and sages bowing to her. In a devotional account, even Shiva received his powers through her and became Ardhanarishvara, half Shiva and half Shakti: one whole. Yet the tradition keeps saying powers are not the goal. The Yoga Sutras warn they can become obstacles, and the Devi Mahatmya calls the Goddess “the cause of liberation”. The deepest gift of the ninth night is a discovery, not a power. In the Devi Gita, the Goddess teaches that the self in every being is one with the infinite, and that knowing this sets you free. An old image for it is a wave discovering it was always the ocean. After eight nights of rootedness, resolve, readiness, creation, nurture, courage, fearlessness and renewal, the journey ends there: not with becoming stronger, but with no longer feeling separate.

## Night by night

### Night 1: Shailaputri, daughter of the mountain

**The story (Tradition).** Sati, daughter of Daksha, marries Shiva. Her father agrees only because Brahma asks him to, and never accepts Shiva. Daksha holds a great sacrifice and invites every god except Shiva. Sati goes anyway, against Shiva's advice. Daksha insults Shiva in front of the assembly. Sati refuses to keep the body her father gave her, and leaves it through yogic fire. Then comes Shiva's grief: Virabhadra destroys the sacrifice, and Shiva wanders, carrying her body. She is reborn as Parvati, daughter of Himavat (the Himalaya) and Mena: Shailaputri. She rides a bull and holds a trident and a lotus.

- **Sources:** Bhagavata Purana 4.3–4.7 (Shiva's warning in 4.3; she leaves her body through yogic fire in 4.4; Virabhadra in 4.5); Shiva Purana, Rudra Samhita (Sati Khanda); Devi Bhagavata Purana book 7. Shiva carrying her body, and the Shakti Peethas, come from later texts (Kalika Purana, Devi Bhagavata) and regional tradition.
- **Ideas:** honouring a bond your family scorns; contempt has consequences (Daksha's pride ruins his own sacrifice); the mountain as a steady place to begin again (Interpretation: the Muladhara, or root, chakra).
- **Primary scene: Aunt Marge (PoA ch. 2, "Aunt Marge's Big Mistake"; PoA film).** Harry has promised to behave all week so Uncle Vernon will sign his Hogsmeade form. At dinner, Marge insults his dead parents. He loses control, inflates her, and leaves on the Knight Bus.
- **Similar:** an elder publicly demeans someone you love at a family gathering. Do you stay silent, speak, or leave?
- **Different, and must not be overlooked:** Harry's reaction is uncontrolled and comic. Sati's act is deliberate, sacred and tragic. Never equate her leaving her body with Harry leaving the house. The useful comparison is the dilemma, not the response.
- **Second scene: the Sorting (PS ch. 7; CoS ch. 18, "Dobby's Reward").** Harry begs not to be put in Slytherin. A year later, Dumbledore tells him that his choices show who he is more than his abilities. The link: Shailaputri begins again, and who you are is not only what your father made you.
- **Optional, for the parent:** Shiva's grief turning into destruction has an echo in Harry wrecking Dumbledore's office after Sirius dies (OotP ch. 37). Intense; use only if the conversation goes there.
- **Strength:** partial. **Intensity:** gentle. The spoiler notice comes before this night. Sati's story needs the "For the parent" note on self-harm, and on the later historical practice that shares her name.

### Night 2: Brahmacharini, the one who practises

**The story (Tradition).** Shiva, grieving Sati, sits in deep meditation. Kama's arrow tries to stir him, and Shiva's third eye burns Kama to ash. Parvati sees that charm will not reach Shiva. She begins severe tapas, at last refusing even leaves (hence the name "Aparna"). She is tested twice: first by the seven sages, then by Shiva himself in disguise, who mocks Shiva. She argues back and turns to leave, and he reveals himself. She carries a rosary and a water pot, and walks barefoot in white.

- **Sources:** Shiva Purana, Rudra Samhita (Parvati Khanda). Kalidasa's *Kumarasambhava* canto 5 is a classical poem, not scripture; label it so if used.
- **Ideas:** sustained effort; changing approach after a failure; holding steady when someone tests your resolve.
- **Primary scene: the Patronus lessons (PoA ch. 12, "The Patronus"; ch. 20, "The Dementor's Kiss"; ch. 21, "Hermione's Secret"; PoA film).** Harry collapses in practice again and again, and keeps going. At the lake he is overwhelmed. When he returns by Time-Turner he succeeds, because he has already seen himself do it.
- **Similar:** repeated failure, continued practice, and confidence earned through effort.
- **Different:** Harry wants a protective skill and has a teacher. Parvati's tapas is a spiritual discipline, undertaken against her mother's wishes. The Time-Turner adds a magical twist to Harry's success.
- **Second scene, a contrast: Occlumency (OotP ch. 24, "Occlumency"; ch. 28; ch. 32, "Out of the Fire"; OotP film).** Harry barely practises, partly because he resents Snape and partly because the corridor dreams half-draw him in. The lessons collapse, Voldemort plants a false vision, and Harry rushes to the Ministry, where Sirius dies.
- **Handle with care:** the blame is not Harry's alone. Snape's hostile teaching, Dumbledore's silence and Kreacher's lies all play a part. Don't let the child conclude "Sirius died because Harry didn't practise."
- **Gentle alternative:** Hermione drills Harry in the Summoning Charm until about 2 a.m. (GoF ch. 20). This is in the book only; the film shows only the summons during the task.
- **Strength:** strong. **Intensity:** some peril; the second scene is intense.

### Night 3: Chandraghanta, the moon-bell

**The story.** She wears a half moon shaped like a bell on her forehead, has ten arms, and rides a tiger or lion. The sound of her bell drives away demons. **Tradition:** in Devi Mahatmya ch. 4 (verse 24), the gods ask the Devi to protect them with the sound of her bell. **Popular devotional telling:** at the wedding, Shiva arrives in a terrifying procession and Parvati's mother Mena faints. Parvati takes this fierce form, and Shiva is persuaded to appear gently (in the Shiva Purana by Narada; in popular telling by Parvati). Mena's fainting is in the Shiva Purana (Rudra Samhita, Parvati Khanda, ch. 43), but that text does not call the form Chandraghanta. The link is devotional.

- **Ideas:** fierce readiness that protects; calm (the moon) and alarm (the bell) together; standing between two sides you love.
- **Primary scene: Neville stands up to his friends (PS ch. 16, "Through the Trapdoor"; ch. 17; PS film).** Neville blocks the trio from leaving at night and says he will fight them. Hermione freezes him with a spell. Later, Dumbledore awards him ten points for the courage of standing up to his friends.
- **Similar:** someone stands in the way to protect their community, at personal cost, against their own side.
- **Different:** Neville is frightened, loses, and is wrong about what is at stake. Chandraghanta's strength is divine and unshaken. Most pre-teens will recognise Neville's situation from their own friendships.
- **Second scene: Molly Weasley's duel (DH ch. 36, "The Flaw in the Plan"; DH Part 2 film).** A curse narrowly misses Ginny. Mrs Weasley, the warmest person in the series, takes over the fight herself and kills Bellatrix.
- **Similar:** gentleness and ferocity in one person; ferocity that rises only to protect.
- **Different:** Molly's fury is personal and ends in a killing. The Devi's ferocity restores order rather than taking revenge, and Chandraghanta's calm never leaves her.
- **Gentle alternative:** Harry casts a Patronus to save Dudley, who bullies him, knowing he could be expelled for it (OotP ch. 1; OotP film).
- **Strength:** partial to strong. **Intensity:** gentle; the second scene is intense (a killing in battle).

### Night 4: Kushmanda, the warm cosmic egg

**The story (devotional tradition, not a Puranic narrative).** Before creation there was only darkness. Kushmanda smiled, and the cosmic egg (brahmanda) appeared. She is said to live in the core of the sun and give it light. She has eight arms and rides a lion. Her name is often explained as *ku* (a little) + *ushma* (warmth) + *anda* (egg). *Kushmanda* is also the ash gourd offered to her. **Wider background (Tradition):** the golden embryo, Hiranyagarbha, in Rig Veda 10.121.

- **Sources:** the smile and the cosmic egg come from devotional tradition (e.g. Gita Press's *Nava Durga*), not from a Purana. The Devi Kavacham gives only her name.
- **Ideas:** creation as an act of joy; light that sustains others; a small warmth with very large effects.
- **Primary scene: Hagrid's birthday cake (PS ch. 4, "The Keeper of the Keys"; PS film).** At midnight, in a hut on a rock, a giant brings Harry a squashed cake. No one has ever marked his birthday before, and his new world begins.
- **Second scene: the Christmas jumper (PS ch. 12; PS film).** Mrs Weasley makes a jumper for a boy she has met once.
- **Similar:** a small act of warmth in the dark changes what is possible for someone.
- **Different:** this is a cosmic creation story, and nothing in Harry Potter creates a world. Hagrid delivers news; he does not make anything. The link rests on a feeling, not a decision.
- **Strength:** speculative. **Intensity:** gentle.

### Night 5: Skandamata, mother of Skanda

**The story (Tradition, combined version, labelled as such).** The demon Taraka has a boon: only a son of Shiva can defeat him. Shiva's fiery spark is too powerful to hold. Agni carries it, then the river Ganga, and it is laid in a forest of reeds. Six star-mothers, the Krittikas, nurse the child, and he grows six faces. Parvati embraces him as her own. He leads the gods' army and defeats Taraka. Skandamata holds the infant Skanda on her lap and lotuses in two hands, and rides a lion.

- **Sources:** Shiva Purana, Rudra Samhita (Kumara Khanda); Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda (Ganga version); Mahabharata, Vana Parva (a different version, with Svaha). **Tamil tradition:** Parvati gives Murugan the *vel* (spear). Thaipusam honours the vel, and Skanda Sashti marks his defeat of the demon Surapadman.
- **Ideas:** protecting a child; letting a child go to meet their purpose (she holds him on her lap, yet arms him for battle); a child raised by many hands.
- **Primary scene: Molly and Sirius argue (OotP ch. 5, "The Order of the Phoenix"; OotP film, shortened).** At the table after dinner, Harry demands to know what is happening. Sirius thinks he has a right to know. Mrs Weasley says he is still a child, and not Sirius's to treat as James. Lupin mediates, and Harry is told a little.
- **Similar:** exactly the dilemma of how much to protect, and when to let a young person face danger.
- **Different:** Molly is not Harry's mother; she is one of many carers, rather like the Krittikas. Skanda was born for his battle. Harry's danger is real, and the adults genuinely disagree.
- **Second scene: Narcissa's lie (DH ch. 36; DH Part 2 film).** Voldemort sends Narcissa Malfoy to check whether Harry is dead. She feels his heartbeat and whispers to ask whether Draco is alive. When Harry says yes, she tells Voldemort that Harry is dead.
- **Similar:** a mother's protection that outweighs every other loyalty.
- **Different:** Narcissa protects her own child at the expense of her side's cause, and saves Harry almost by accident. Skandamata's protection serves the good of the world. A good "go deeper" question: is love for your own child always good?
- **Optional:** Lily's protection (PS ch. 17; PS film). For the parent only: Molly's boggart shows her family dead (OotP ch. 9; not in the film).
- **Strength:** strong. **Intensity:** gentle; the second scene has some peril.

### Night 6: Katyayani, born of the gods' combined fury

**The story (Tradition).** The buffalo-demon Mahishasura defeats the gods and seizes heaven. When the gods hear, their anger blazes out as light. The lights merge into one radiant Devi, and each god gives her a weapon: Shiva a trident, Vishnu a discus, Indra a thunderbolt and a bell, Himavat a lion. She battles his army and kills Mahishasura.

- **Sources:** Devi Mahatmya ch. 2–3 (Markandeya Purana): the gods' energies become one woman (ch. 2), and Mahishasura is slain (ch. 3). The Vamana Purana places her emergence at the hermitage of the sage Katyayana, hence "Katyayani"; in a Kalika Purana variant, Katyayana is simply her first worshipper. Bhagavata Purana 10.22: the gopis pray to Katyayani. In Navaratri practice she is often identified with Mahishasuramardini.
- **Ideas:** separate powers joined to one purpose; anger turned into action against injustice; each person gives what they have.
- **Primary scene: Dumbledore's Army is formed (OotP ch. 16, "In the Hog's Head"; ch. 18, "Dumbledore's Army"; OotP film).** Umbridge refuses to teach practical defence, and the Ministry denies that Voldemort has returned. Students who could do nothing alone meet in secret, sign up, and train in the Room of Requirement. Ginny suggests the name.
- **Similar:** many people pool their will against an injustice, and a shared grievance becomes a disciplined practice.
- **Different:** the gods give their power to one figure who acts, whereas DA members each grow their own. The DA breaks school rules, which makes a good "go deeper" question: was that justified? The Devi's battle ends in a death; the DA's purpose is defence.
- **Second scene: past the trapdoor (PS ch. 16; the PS film omits the potion riddle).** Hermione beats the Devil's Snare and the logic puzzle, Harry catches the flying key, and Ron wins the chess game at his own cost. None of them could have got through alone.
- **Optional:** in the Battle of Hogwarts, house-elves, centaurs and Hogsmeade villagers all join the fight (DH ch. 36). It is the closest image of many different powers converging, and it is intense.
- **Strength:** strong. **Intensity:** gentle.

### Night 7: Kalaratri, the dark night

**The story (Tradition).** Kali springs from the Devi's brow to defeat the generals Chanda and Munda (Devi Mahatmya ch. 7). Then comes Raktabija, whose every spilled drop of blood becomes a new demon. Chamunda drinks the blood before it can fall, and he is defeated (ch. 8). "Kalaratri" is one of the Devi's names in Brahma's hymn (ch. 1, verse 75). In Navaratri worship she is dark, with loose hair, three eyes and fiery breath, and she rides a donkey. Two of her hands hold weapons; the other two show *abhaya* ("do not fear") and *varada* (giving). She is also called Shubhankari, "the one who does good". The link between Kalaratri and Raktabija in particular is devotional tradition.

- **Ideas:** a terrifying form with protective hands; some problems multiply when you hit them head-on and need another approach; darkness is not the same as evil.
- **Primary scene: the boggart lesson (PoA ch. 7, "The Boggart in the Wardrobe"; ch. 8, "Flight of the Fat Lady"; PoA film).** The class faces its worst fears and turns them ridiculous. Lupin steps in front of Harry's boggart. Later he tells Harry that fearing the dementor most means he fears fear itself; the film keeps this almost unchanged.
- **Similar:** fear is faced, not avoided, and a guardian stands between a child and the thing he fears.
- **Different, and the real lesson:** Riddikulus *shrinks* a fear by making it silly. Kalaratri is never made smaller. Her fearsomeness is itself the protection, and she is revered, not laughed at.
- **Second scene: "The Prince's Tale" (DH ch. 33; DH Part 2 film).** Snape's memories show that the cold, frightening teacher spent years protecting Harry.
- **Similar:** a terrifying exterior hides a protective purpose.
- **Different, and crucial:** Kalaratri is fearsome *and* wholly good; her fierceness is her kindness. Snape was also genuinely cruel, to Neville, to Hermione and to Harry, and protection does not cancel cruelty. This is the best "go deeper" discussion of the nine nights.
- **Gentle alternative:** the black dog Harry takes for the Grim, an omen of death, turns out to be his godfather, Sirius (PoA ch. 17–19; PoA film).
- **Strength:** strong. **Intensity:** some peril; the second scene is intense (it follows Snape's death).
- **Rejected:** the curse in Gringotts that multiplies treasure (DH ch. 26). It resembles Raktabija on the surface only.

### Night 8: Mahagauri, radiant and white

**The story.** There are several tellings. **Tradition:** in the Skanda Purana (Prabhasa Khanda, ch. 68), Shiva teasingly calls the Devi (as Sati, in her earlier birth) "Kali", the dark one. Hurt, she does tapas and becomes Gauri, the bright one, by her own effort. In the Kalika Purana, Shiva bathes her in the heavenly Ganga and gives her a golden glow. **Popular Navaratri telling:** after long tapas her body is dark with dust and sun, and Shiva washes her in the Ganga until she shines white. Devi Mahatmya ch. 5 runs the other way: Kaushiki emerges from Parvati's body, and Parvati herself becomes dark, as Kalika. Mahagauri wears white, rides a bull, and holds a trident and a small drum. Many families honour young girls (Kanya Puja) on this day.

- **For the parent:** the "dark to fair" story can be heard as "fair is pure". Traditional readings concern inner purification, not skin. Kalaratri, who is dark, is worshipped the night before with equal reverence, and in the Devi Mahatmya it is Parvati who becomes dark. Say this plainly.
- **Ideas:** renewal after long effort; purity as inner clarity; gentleness after the ferocity of Kalaratri.
- **Primary scene: Kreacher (DH ch. 10, "Kreacher's Tale"; ch. 12, "Magic is Might"; book only).** Harry listens to Kreacher's story and gives him a locket that belonged to his old master, Regulus. Kreacher is transformed: clean, cooking, loyal. The films leave this out, so it may be new even to a child who knows them well.
- **Similar:** restoration after long suffering; an outward brightness that shows an inner change.
- **Different:** Kreacher's change is sparked by someone else's kindness, not by his own tapas. The books leave the house-elves' servitude unresolved, which is a hard question in itself.
- **Second scene: Ron returns (DH ch. 19, "The Silver Doe"; DH Part 1 film).** Ron, who walked out on his friends, finds his way back. He pulls Harry from a frozen pool and destroys the locket, which first shows him his worst fears. Hermione is furious; Harry forgives quickly.
- **Similar:** renewal after a dark stretch, earned by facing what darkened you.
- **Different:** Ron's darkness came from the locket and his own jealousy. Mahagauri's tapas is not atonement for doing wrong.
- **Go deeper:** Harry urges Voldemort to feel remorse, and he refuses (DH ch. 36; book only). Renewal can be offered, but it has to be chosen.
- **Strength:** partial to strong. **Intensity:** gentle; the second scene has some peril.

### Night 9: Siddhidatri, the giver of perfection

**The story.** She grants *siddhis*, traditionally eight perfections such as becoming very small or very light. **Devotional tradition:** Shiva worshipped her and received the siddhis, and half his body became hers: Ardhanarishvara. Gods, sages, demons and humans all worship her. She sits on a lotus and holds a discus, conch, mace and lotus.

- **Sources:** devotional tradition. No Purana tells of the Ardhanarishvara link; modern accounts say she is "believed to have conferred" the siddhis.
- **What follows (Tradition and regional practice):** Vijayadashami, or Dussehra. In the north, Rama's victory over Ravana and the burning of effigies. In Bengal, Bijoya Dashami and the immersion of Durga. In the south, Ayudha Puja on the ninth day honours tools, books and vehicles, and on Vijayadashami in Kerala children write their first letters (Vidyarambham). Golu displays in the south and garba in Gujarat run across all nine nights.
- **Ideas:** power as a gift held lightly; completion; honouring the tools of your work.
- **Primary scene: the Elder Wand (DH ch. 35, "King's Cross"; ch. 36; DH Part 2 film).** At King's Cross, Dumbledore suggests that those best suited to power are those who never sought it. Harry, now master of the most powerful wand ever made, uses it only to mend his own broken wand, and decides to return it to Dumbledore's tomb.
- **Film difference:** in the film, Harry snaps the Elder Wand and throws it away (confirmed; whether the film shows the mended wand is still to check). Keeping power unused and destroying it are two different ideas of giving it up, and the difference is worth discussing.
- **Similar:** the right relationship with power is choosing not to hold it.
- **Different:** Siddhidatri is the *source* of power and gives it away; Harry holds power and lays it down.
- **Second scene: the Stone and the Mirror (PS ch. 12; ch. 17; PS film).** Dumbledore designed the Mirror so that only someone who wanted to find the Stone, but not use it, could take it. Harry can; Quirrell cannot.
- **Strength:** strong. **Intensity:** gentle.

## Honest notes on weak nights

**Night 4, Kushmanda (speculative).** Hers is a cosmic creation story told mainly in devotional tradition, and nothing in Harry Potter creates a world. The cake and the jumper match a feeling, not a decision. Recommendation: let the story lead, then hold a family discussion of small things that started big things for us. Hagrid's cake becomes an optional two-minute coda. An optional extra is a conversation about how the universe began, clearly labelled as a different kind of knowledge from a creation story. (Dropped on 18 October 2026: each night keeps one theme only.)

**Night 1, Shailaputri (partial).** Sati's central act has no Harry Potter parallel, and it should not have one. Only the dilemma matches: what to do when family scorns someone you love.

**Night 8, Mahagauri (partial to strong).** Kreacher's change is in the books only, so a child who knows the films best will need more of it retold. The colourism risk needs careful handling.

**Night 3, Chandraghanta (partial to strong).** Neville fits "standing up to your own side" well, but the protective bell less well. Molly's duel is the closest match to "calm and fierce together", but it ends in a killing, so it comes second and carries a parent note.

**Overall framing.** Each night presents the "nine steps of inner growth" reading as a Contemporary frame, not ancient teaching. The nine colours of Navaratri began in 2003 as a *Maharashtra Times* newspaper campaign, so mention them only with that label.

**One chapter carries a lot.** DH ch. 36 supplies scenes on Nights 3, 5 and 9, with optional extras on Nights 6 and 8. Each night should retell its moment fresh rather than assume the child remembers the chapter.

**Corrections made while writing Nights 2–9 (the night files are the final word):**

- **Night 2:** the Shiva Purana has two tests. First the seven sages test her (Parvati Khanda ch. 25), then Shiva himself, disguised as an old ascetic (ch. 26–28). Kalidasa makes him a young brahmacharin. Parvati argues back and turns to leave, rather than blocking her ears. The "u ma" naming is in *Kumarasambhava* canto 1, verse 26.
- **Night 3:** in the Shiva Purana, Narada (urged by Vishnu) persuades Shiva to take a beautiful form. Parvati asking him is the popular telling.
- **Night 5:** in the Shiva Purana the spark also passes through the sages' wives and Himavat. There the Krittikas are six women who come to bathe; identifying them as the Pleiades comes from the Ramayana.
- **Night 8:** in the Skanda Purana (Prabhasa Khanda ch. 68), the Devi is Sati in her earlier birth, and she becomes fair through her own tapas, with no bath. The heavenly-Ganga bath is from the Kalika Purana (ch. 47, via Hazra's summary), where she is named Kali.
- **Night 9:** Ayudha Puja falls on the ninth day (Mahanavami); Vidyarambham is on Vijayadashami. The Mirror scene is rated partial to strong, and the Elder Wand strong.
