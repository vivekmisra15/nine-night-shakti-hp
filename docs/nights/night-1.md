# Night 1: Shailaputri, daughter of the mountain

The lesson and the read-aloud script for Night 1. The plan for all nine nights is in `docs/NINE_NIGHTS_CONTENT.md`.

# Lesson

Night 1 takes about 30 minutes, or about 20 on the short path. Its question: what do you do when your own family scorns someone you love? The night tells Sati's story and Shailaputri's new beginning, then two Harry Potter scenes: Aunt Marge's dinner and the Sorting.

- **Intensity:** gentle. Sati's death is told gently, as a sacred act and not an example. Read the parent note in Beat 5 before you begin.

## Beat 1: Before we begin

**Spoiler notice (shown once, before Night 1):** these nine nights discuss the whole Harry Potter series, all seven books and all eight films, including the biggest twists and the deaths of important characters. If you haven't finished the series yet, you may want to wait.

**How we compare (shown once):** Harry Potter is a story one author wrote. The Devi is worshipped by millions of people today. We are not saying any character *is* a Devi, or that the two stories mean the same thing. We look for moments where someone faces a similar choice, and then we look just as hard at what is different.

**What Navaratri is.**

- **Tradition:** *Nava* means nine and *ratri* means night. The main Navaratri falls in the autumn month of Ashvin (September or October). On each night the Devi is worshipped in one of nine forms, the Navadurga. The nine names come from the Devi Kavacham, a hymn recited with the Devi Mahatmya, which tells how the Devi defeated the buffalo-demon Mahishasura.
- **Regional traditions:** families keep it differently. Gujarat has garba and dandiya dancing, Bengal has Durga Puja, South India has Golu displays of dolls, and North India has Ramlila plays. Many homes begin on Night 1 by setting up a sacred pot (Ghatasthapana) and sowing grain that sprouts over the nine days. The festival ends with Vijayadashami, or Dussehra.
- **Contemporary:** many people today read the nine nights as a journey of inner growth, one quality per night. It is a helpful modern frame, not an ancient teaching, and we use it lightly.

## The traditional story (Beats 2–8)

This follows the Bhagavata Purana (book 4, chapters 2–7) and the Shiva Purana (Rudra Samhita). Where later texts add to it, the beat says so.

### Beat 2: The daughter who chose (tradition)

Daksha was one of the great lords of creation, proud of his rank and his rituals. Among his daughters was Sati, and Sati loved Shiva. Shiva was the ascetic of the mountains: smeared with ash, wearing snakes, followed by ghosts and wild spirits, and caring nothing for wealth or rank. Daksha gave Sati to Shiva in marriage only because Brahma asked him to, and he never accepted his son-in-law. (Some popular tellings say he forbade the marriage outright.) Sati and Shiva lived happily on Mount Kailasa.

### Beat 3: A sacrifice without Shiva (tradition)

At a great gathering, everyone rose to greet Daksha except Brahma and Shiva. Daksha took it as an insult. In front of the assembly he abused Shiva and declared that Shiva should never again receive a share of the sacrificial offerings. Later, Daksha held a grand sacrifice and invited every god except Shiva. Sati saw the wives of the gods passing overhead in fine clothes on their way to her father's house. She longed to see her mother and sisters, and the home she grew up in.

### Beat 4: Should Sati go? (choice, hypothetical)

**Set-up:** Sati asks Shiva to go with her. Surely a daughter needs no invitation to her father's house? Shiva says you may visit family uninvited, but not a host who is angry with you. He warns that harsh words from a relative wound deeper than an enemy's arrows, and that she will not be honoured there. (BP 4.3)

**Ask:** If you were Sati, what would you do?

| Option | Acknowledgement shown |
| --- | --- |
| Go: it's my family, and I miss my mother and sisters | Family pulls hard, and an invitation isn't the only reason to turn up. That's Sati's own argument. |
| Stay: Shiva knows how my father treats him | You trust the warning of someone who has been hurt before. That's Shiva's argument. |
| Go, and try to make peace | Hoping to mend things is generous. Notice that peace needs the other side to want it too. |
| Send a message first | A careful middle path. What would the message say, and would Daksha read it kindly? |

**What happens:** Sati went.

**Activity (4 minutes; this is the night's main activity):** one person argues as Sati for going and the other as Shiva for staying, 90 seconds each. Then swap roles and do it again. Afterwards, ask: what did you hear in the other side's argument once you had to make it yourself?

### Beat 5: At her father's house (tradition)

Her mother and sisters ran to welcome her with tears of joy. Her father did not speak to her at all. She looked around and saw that no share of the offering had been set aside for Shiva. Shiva's attendants wanted to attack Daksha, and Sati stopped them. Then she spoke in front of everyone. Shiva is a friend to every being, she said, and has no enemies; her father hunted for faults where Shiva would look for good. She said she was ashamed that her body had come from such a father, and that she would not keep it. She sat down facing north, closed her eyes, and went into deep meditation with her mind fixed on Shiva. Through the power of that meditation a fire rose from within, and she left her body. A great cry went up across the worlds, and everyone who watched blamed Daksha. (BP 4.4)

**Visual:** a single lamp flame rising and going out. No body, and no fire around a figure.

**For the parent (show before this beat):**

- This is a sacred story about a Devi, and the texts treat her act as hers alone. It is not a model for how people respond to being hurt.
- Say plainly, even if it feels obvious, that if anyone ever hurts or humiliates you, the answer is to tell someone you trust and get help, never to harm yourself.
- The historical practice called "sati" shares her name but is a different thing. In the story Sati was not a widow, and Shiva was alive. The practice of widows dying on a husband's funeral pyre was banned in 1829 (the Bengal Sati Regulation), and again in independent India by the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987. Hindu reformers such as Raja Ram Mohan Roy campaigned against it.
- If your child seems troubled by this part, pause the night. The rest can wait.

### Beat 6: Shiva's grief (tradition, combined telling)

The sage Narada brought Shiva the news, and Shiva's grief became fury. He tore a lock of his hair and dashed it down, and from it rose Virabhadra, a terrible warrior, who destroyed Daksha's sacrifice (BP 4.5). In later texts, the Devi Bhagavata Purana and the Kalika Purana, Shiva lifts Sati's body and wanders the world, lost in grief. Vishnu gently separates the body piece by piece, and wherever a piece falls a Shakti Peetha arises. These sacred places are still visited today, for example Kamakhya in Assam and Kalighat in Kolkata. At last Shiva withdraws into deep, silent meditation in the mountains.

### Beat 7: What will Shiva do with his grief? (choice, hypothetical)

**Ask:** Shiva has lost Sati because of Daksha's contempt. What do you think he does?

| Option | Acknowledgement shown |
| --- | --- |
| Punish Daksha | Anger at a terrible wrong. The story agrees: this comes first. |
| Forgive him | Much harder. The story gets there too, later. |
| Go away and be alone | Grief often wants silence. He does withdraw. |
| Hold on to Sati | Love that can't let go. Later texts tell exactly this. |

**What happens:** all four, in different texts. In the Bhagavata, the gods plead with Shiva and he relents. He restores the sacrifice and brings Daksha back to life with a goat's head in place of his own, and Daksha weeps and asks forgiveness (BP 4.7). Grief, like this story, can be more than one thing at once.

### Beat 8: Daughter of the mountain (tradition)

Himavat, king of the mountains, and his wife Mena prayed for a daughter, and Sati resolved to be born to them (Shiva Purana, Rudra Samhita, Parvati Khanda ch. 5). The Bhagavata also says she was reborn to Mena in the Himalayas and chose Shiva again (BP 4.7.58–59). She was Parvati, "she of the mountain". On this first night she is worshipped as Shailaputri: *shaila*, mountain, and *putri*, daughter. She is the same Devi with a new birth, in a family that honours the one she loves. Meanwhile Shiva sits in meditation, unaware of her, and tomorrow's story begins there.

**Hotspots (tap to learn), all labelled interpretation:**

| Symbol | Traditional meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Bull (Nandi) | Steadiness, and strength under control; often linked with dharma. The bull is Shiva's companion, so she arrives already bound to him. |
| Trident (in her right hand) | Often read as the three qualities of nature (gunas), or as creation, preservation and dissolution. |
| Lotus (in her left hand) | It grows clean out of mud: purity in the middle of the world. |
| Crescent moon | Calm and coolness; also Shiva's own ornament. |
| The mountain in her name | Steadiness and roots. In yogic interpretation, Night 1 is linked with the Muladhara, or root, chakra. |

**Did you know? (tradition and interpretation):** in the Kena Upanishad (3.12–4.1), "Uma Haimavati", Uma of the Himalaya, appears to the god Indra and teaches the proud gods that their victory was not really their own. Tradition identifies her with Parvati. So this is a daughter of the mountain who corrects pride, on the night of a story about a proud father.

## Beat 9: Three ideas (interpretation)

Each idea shows the simple version first, then the deeper one, then the story moments that support it.

**1. Standing by someone others scorn**

- **Simply:** when people close to you look down on someone you love, do you stand with that person?
- **From the story:** Sati defends Shiva before the whole assembly, in her own father's house. Even in her anger, she stops Shiva's attendants from attacking her father.
- **Traditional reading:** Shiva and Shakti cannot be separated, so an insult to Shiva is an insult to her. Devotional readings hold up Sati's loyalty as a model of devotion.
- **Other readings:** Sati was loyal to both sides; she went because she loved her family too. Was going brave, or a mistake Shiva had foreseen? It can be both.

**2. Contempt costs the person who holds it**

- **Simply:** looking down on people harms you as well as them.
- **From the story:** Daksha's pride costs him his daughter, his sacrifice and his own head.
- **Traditional reading:** a sacrifice that leaves out the divine at its centre cannot be complete. Shiva himself warns that pride makes people blind (BP 4.3).
- **Other readings:** Daksha may have believed he was defending proper order, because Shiva ignored the customs Daksha lived by. Pride can look like principle. And even Daksha is given a second chance.

**3. Beginning again from steady ground**

- **Simply:** after a loss, you can start again without stopping being yourself.
- **From the story:** Sati returns as Parvati, daughter of the mountain: the same Devi, a new family, the same love.
- **Traditional reading (interpretation):** Shailaputri is the root of the nine nights, so the journey begins from steady ground.
- **Contemporary:** think of a fresh start, such as a new school or a new home. What do you carry with you, and what do you leave behind?

## Beat 10: Aunt Marge's dinner (hogwarts)

- **Reference:** Harry. *Prisoner of Azkaban*, chapter 2, "Aunt Marge's Big Mistake". Film: *Prisoner of Azkaban* (2004), opening scenes.
- **Watch the scene:** `video: { type: "search", url: "https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Harry+Potter+Prisoner+of+Azkaban+Aunt+Marge+dinner+scene" }`. Swap in an exact clip when one is chosen.
- **Film note:** the film keeps the insults, the inflating, and Harry leaving on the Knight Bus. Compare how the film handles the deal over the Hogsmeade form with the book before quoting details.

**Retelling (spoken style).** It's the summer Harry turns thirteen. Uncle Vernon's sister Marge comes to stay for a week with her bulldog, and Vernon makes Harry a deal. If Harry behaves, never mentions magic, and goes along with the story that he attends a school for hopeless criminal boys, Vernon will sign the form that lets him visit the wizarding village of Hogsmeade. So Harry grits his teeth. All week Marge sneers at him, and he says nothing. On the last night, after a long dinner and a lot of drink, she turns on his parents. She talks about breeding dogs: if something is wrong with the mother, something is wrong with the pup. She calls his father a jobless good-for-nothing, and repeats the Dursleys' lie that his parents died drunk in a car crash. Harry says they didn't, and she keeps going. Harry's anger rises past the point he can hold it, and his magic escapes him: Marge swells up like a balloon and floats to the ceiling. Harry drags his trunk out of the house and into the dark street, certain he'll be expelled. The form is never signed.

**The connection:** Idea 1, standing by someone others scorn, and Idea 2, contempt.

- **What is similar:** an elder insults absent loved ones at a family gathering, after contempt that has been building for a long time. The rest of the household stays silent, as the guests at Daksha's sacrifice did out of fear of him. Aunt Petunia says nothing, though Marge is insulting her own sister, Lily.
- **What is different:**
    - Harry doesn't choose his response; his magic escapes him. The story treats Sati's act as deliberate and sacred.
    - The scene is partly comic; Sati's story is a tragedy.
    - Harry is a powerless child in that house. Sati is a Devi, speaking to her own father.
    - Magic in Harry Potter is a learned and inherited skill. Shakti is understood as divine power itself.
    - Most important: Harry leaving the house is not like Sati leaving her body, and we never treat it as if it were.
- **Why the comparison helps:** it brings Sati's dilemma down to a scale a pre-teen knows: a dinner table where someone you love is being mocked.
- **Strength: partial.** The dilemma matches closely; the responses deliberately don't.

## Beat 11: Think it through (comparison)

**The ladder**

1. **Notice:** what deal had Harry made, and what was he trying to protect by keeping quiet all week?
2. **Feel:** what do you think Harry felt as Marge kept talking? Where did it change?
3. **Wonder:** why do you think Vernon and Petunia said nothing?
4. **Stretch:** Harry didn't decide to inflate her. Is he still responsible for what happened?
5. **Turn it around:** tell the dinner from Aunt Petunia's side. Marge was insulting her sister too.
6. **Connect:** how is Harry at that table like Sati at her father's sacrifice, and how is he completely different?

**If they say…, try asking…**

- "She deserved it" → "Did she deserve it, or did Harry want it? Are those the same thing?"
- "He should have ignored her" → "Would you ignore it if someone talked like that about me?"
- "It wasn't his fault, it was accidental magic" → "Do we have our own 'accidental magic', things that come out before we decide?"
- "Sati and Harry both just got angry" → "Did Sati do anything before she spoke? (She stopped the attendants from attacking.) What does that tell you?"

**Stance line:** "Harry should have kept his temper: the deal mattered" ↔ "Some insults have to be answered, whatever it costs". Each person places a named marker, then explains it in one sentence.

**Activity: three seconds before (3 minutes, with a visible timer).** Freeze at the moment Marge calls Harry's father a good-for-nothing. Each person has 60 seconds to suggest one thing Harry could say or do that defends his parents *and* keeps his control. Then judge each other's ideas: which protects his parents best, and which protects Harry best? Are they the same?

## Beat 12: The Sorting (hogwarts)

- **Reference:** Harry. *Philosopher's Stone*, chapter 7, "The Sorting Hat", with *Chamber of Secrets*, chapter 18, "Dobby's Reward". Films: *Philosopher's Stone* (2001), the Sorting; *Chamber of Secrets* (2002), Dumbledore's office at the end. The film keeps the "choices" idea, though its wording may differ from the book's.
- **Watch the scenes:** `video: { type: "search", url: "https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Harry+Potter+Philosopher%27s+Stone+Sorting+Hat+Harry+not+Slytherin" }` and `video: { type: "search", url: "https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Harry+Potter+Chamber+of+Secrets+Dumbledore+choices+scene" }`.

**Retelling (spoken style).** It's Harry's first night at Hogwarts. The new students are called up one by one to sit on a stool and put on an old, patched hat that decides their house. Harry already has an opinion. Hagrid has told him that every witch or wizard who went bad came from Slytherin, and on the train he met Draco Malfoy, who sneered at Ron's family and was sure he'd be a Slytherin. Now the Hat speaks inside Harry's head. It sees courage, a good mind, talent and a hunger to prove himself, and says Slytherin could help him to greatness. Harry silently begs: not Slytherin. After a pause, the Hat calls out Gryffindor.

A year later Harry is troubled. He can talk to snakes, as Salazar Slytherin could, the Hat had said he'd do well in Slytherin, and Tom Riddle told him they were alike. Dumbledore reminds him that the Hat put him in Gryffindor because he *asked* not to go to Slytherin. Our choices, Dumbledore says, show who we really are, far more than our abilities.

**The connection:** Idea 3, beginning again, and the night's question of who you are.

- **What is similar:** in the Bhagavata, Sati can no longer bear being called "Dakshayani", Daksha's daughter (BP 4.4.23). She returns as the mountain's daughter, in a family that honours what she loves. Harry refuses to be defined by what he shares with Voldemort. Both stories separate what you were given from who you are.
- **What is different:**
    - Sati's rebirth is a divine act in a sacred story. Harry is an eleven-year-old picking a school house.
    - Harry's choice rests on rumours from Hagrid and Malfoy. The series later complicates Slytherin through Snape, Regulus Black and Slughorn, and in the epilogue Harry tells his own son that the Hat takes your choice into account.
    - Sati is the same Devi in a new body. Harry stays the same Harry whatever his house.
- **Why the comparison helps:** it turns a big idea, rebirth, into a question a pre-teen can answer: what part of me did I choose?
- **Strength: partial.** The theme of identity matches; the situations are far apart.

## Beat 13: Think it through (comparison)

**The ladder**

1. **Notice:** what reasons did the Hat give for Slytherin? Where did Harry's opinion of Slytherin come from?
2. **Feel:** how might it feel to have the whole hall watching while a hat reads your mind?
3. **Wonder:** was Harry choosing Gryffindor, or running away from Slytherin? Does it matter?
4. **Stretch:** Harry judged a whole house on what two people told him. Knowing about Snape, Regulus and Slughorn, was his choice still right?
5. **Turn it around:** imagine the Hat had put him in Slytherin anyway. What about Harry would have stayed the same?
6. **Connect:** Sati didn't want to be known as "Daksha's daughter". Harry didn't want to be like Voldemort. What can we choose about who we are, and what can't we?

**If they say…, try asking…**

- "Choices matter more than abilities" → "Can you think of a time an ability mattered more? (Hermione's cleverness saves them again and again.)"
- "He was just scared" → "Can a choice made out of fear still be a good one?"
- "Slytherins are the bad ones" → "Name a Slytherin who did something brave."
- "Sati and Harry both escaped their families" → "Did Sati stop loving her mother and sisters? Did Harry stop being Lily's son?"

**Stance line:** "Who you are is mostly what you choose" ↔ "Who you are is mostly what you were given".

**Activity: the Hat's other speech (2 minutes, with a visible timer).** The parent plays the Hat and has 30 seconds to argue for putting the child in the house they would least choose. The child has 30 seconds to answer the Hat. Then swap. Did any argument change your mind, even a little?

## Beat 14: Side by side (comparison)

Questions are revealed one at a time. None has a single right answer.

1. Sati and Harry both sat through an elder insulting someone they loved. What was each one trying to protect?
2. Harry lost control; the story presents Sati's act as chosen. Does it matter whether a response is chosen?
3. Daksha's contempt and Marge's both built up over years. Why do you think nobody stopped them sooner?
4. Shiva's grief turned into destruction, and then into restoring Daksha. After Harry's outburst, the Ministry quietly deflated Marge and changed her memory. Who repairs the damage in each story? Is anything lost when someone else does the repairing?
5. Name one difference between these stories so big that we should never forget it.

**What doesn't compare (always shown):** Sati is a Devi, worshipped today, and her story is sacred. Harry is a fictional boy. We compare the dilemma, not the people.

## Beat 15: Today (contemporary)

There are three situations; the short path uses only the first. Each shows several perspectives a thoughtful person might hold, and none is marked as the right answer.

**1. The joke at the family dinner.** At a big family gathering, an uncle makes fun of your older cousin's new partner, who comes from a different background. Everyone laughs, and your cousin goes quiet.

- "Say something now. Silence looks like agreement."
- "Talk to him privately later. People dig in when they're challenged in public."
- "Tell a parent and let an adult handle it."
- "Let it go this time. It's one evening, and family peace matters too."
- **Ask:** which of these would you be proud of tomorrow? Which would your cousin want?

**2. The group chat.** In a class group chat, your friends start mocking someone you like who isn't in the chat. They are your friends too.

- "Post 'not cool'. One voice can stop it."
- "Leave the chat quietly."
- "Check on the person privately." But they don't know about the chat; would telling them help or hurt?
- "Screenshot it and tell a teacher." That may protect them, and it may cost you friends.
- "Stay out of it. It's not my fight."
- **Ask:** online, nobody sees your face. Does that make staying silent easier, or worse?

**3. Starting again.** You're moving to a new school next term. You could stay exactly who you are, or reinvent yourself.

- "Start fresh. Nobody there knows my embarrassing moments."
- "Keep what matters. Reinventing yourself is a kind of pretending."
- "Both. Parvati was a new birth and the same Devi."
- **Ask:** what is one thing you would keep, no matter where you went?

## Beat 16: Reflection (contemporary)

Pick one or two questions; you don't need them all. The night's main activity, the Sati and Shiva role-play, comes earlier, in Beat 4.

**For the child**

- Shiva warned Sati not to go. Why do you think she went anyway?
- What did Daksha do that was unfair, and when did it start?
- Harry kept quiet for a whole week. At what moment would you have found it hardest?
- If you started again somewhere new, what would you keep?

**Go deeper**

- Daksha probably believed he was defending proper order. How can pride look like principle?
- Sati loved both her father's family and her husband, and they were against each other. Can you be loyal to two people who are against each other?
- Shiva destroyed the sacrifice, then restored Daksha. Which was harder? Can both have been right?
- Harry chose his house based on what other people told him. Is a choice fully yours if other people shaped it?

**Together:** if someone in our family were mocked at a family gathering, what would we want each other to do?

**Private note (saved only on this device):** one thing I want to remember from tonight is…

**Takeaway (shown last):** tonight's story asks a question instead of answering it. When people close to us scorn someone we love, what do we protect, and how? Tomorrow, the daughter of the mountain sets out to reach Shiva, who doesn't notice her at all.

## For the parent

**What to remember**

- The heart of the night is a dilemma, not a moral: what do you do when family scorns someone you love?
- Tell Sati's death gently, and say aloud that it is not an example. Then go straight on to Shiva's grief, so that it reads as tragedy.
- Don't let the child equate Harry walking out with Sati leaving her body. The comparison is about the dilemma only.
- The story doesn't end in revenge: Daksha is restored and repents, and Sati begins again. Point this out.

# Read-aloud script

Read it straight through, or use it alongside the app. It runs about 30 minutes with discussion, or about 20 on the short path. Lines in **[brackets]** are for the parent and are not read aloud. All Harry Potter lines are paraphrased.

## Part 1: Opening and the story (about 12 minutes)

**[Before you start: these nights discuss the whole Harry Potter series, including its ending. Check that's fine for your child.]**

Tonight is the first of nine nights. Navaratri means "nine nights", and on each one people worship the Devi, the great goddess, in a different form. Tonight she is Shailaputri, the daughter of the mountain.

We're going to tell her story the way the old texts tell it. Then we'll look at two moments from Harry Potter where someone faces a choice that's a bit like hers, and in many ways not like hers at all. Harry Potter is a story someone wrote; the Devi is worshipped by millions of people. So we're comparing choices, not saying anyone *is* anyone.

**[Pause: about 1 minute]**

Long ago there was a great lord named Daksha, proud of his rank, his rituals and his rules. He had many daughters, and one of them, Sati, loved Shiva.

Shiva was not the kind of son-in-law Daksha wanted. He lived on a mountain, smeared with ash, with snakes around his neck and wild spirits for company, and he cared nothing for wealth or rank. Daksha agreed to the marriage only because Brahma, the creator, asked him to. In his heart he never accepted Shiva.

One day, at a great gathering, everyone stood up when Daksha walked in. Everyone except Brahma and Shiva. Daksha felt insulted. In front of everyone he spoke harshly about Shiva, and declared that Shiva should never again be given a share of the sacred offerings.

Some time later, Daksha held an enormous ceremony, a sacrifice, and invited every god there was, except Shiva. From her mountain home, Sati watched the wives of the gods flying past in their finest clothes on their way to her father's house. She missed her mother. She missed her sisters. She wanted to go home.

She asked Shiva to come with her. Surely, she said, a daughter doesn't need an invitation to her father's house. Shiva answered gently. You can visit family without an invitation, he said, but not someone who is angry with you. Hard words from family hurt more than an enemy's arrows. You won't be honoured there.

**[PAUSE: Choice. Ask: "If you were Sati, what would you do? Go, stay, go and try to make peace, or send a message first?" Listen to the reasons.]**

**[ACTIVITY: 4 minutes. One of you argues as Sati for going, the other as Shiva for staying, 90 seconds each. Then swap. Ask: "What did you hear in the other side once you had to argue it yourself?"]**

Sati went.

When she arrived, her mother and sisters ran to her with tears of joy. But her father didn't say a word to her. She looked around and saw that there was no share of the offering set aside for Shiva.

Shiva's attendants, who had come with her, wanted to attack Daksha. Sati stopped them. Then she stood in front of everyone and spoke. Shiva is a friend to every living being, she said, and has no enemies. You look for faults; he looks for good. She said she was ashamed that her body had come from such a father, and that she would not keep it.

**[For the parent: read the next part slowly and gently. Afterwards, say in your own words that this is a sacred story about a Devi, not something anyone should ever do, and that if anyone hurts or humiliates us, we tell someone we trust and get help. If your child asks about "sati" as a historical practice, explain that it is a different thing: it was banned in 1829 and again in 1987, and in the story Shiva was alive.]**

Sati sat down, facing north. She closed her eyes and went into a meditation so deep that her whole mind rested on Shiva. Through the power of that meditation, a fire rose from within her, and she left her body. A great cry went up across all the worlds, and everyone who watched turned and looked at Daksha.

**[Pause: give a moment of quiet. Ask: "How are you feeling about that?" Then go on.]**

When Shiva heard, his grief became fury. He tore a lock of his hair and threw it down, and from it rose a terrible warrior, Virabhadra, who smashed Daksha's ceremony to pieces.

Later stories say Shiva lifted Sati's body in his arms and wandered the world, lost in grief. To bring him peace, Vishnu gently separated her body, piece by piece, and wherever a piece fell, a sacred place arose. People still travel to these places today.

**[PAUSE: Choice. Ask: "What do you think Shiva does with his grief? Punish Daksha, forgive him, go away alone, or hold on to Sati?"]**

The answer is all of them, in different tellings. In one of the oldest, the gods begged Shiva for mercy, and he relented. He restored the ceremony and brought Daksha back to life, though with a goat's head in place of his own. Daksha wept and asked Shiva to forgive him. Then Shiva went to the mountains and sat in silent meditation for a very long time.

But that is not the end of Sati. In the Himalayas, the king of the mountains, Himavat, and his wife Mena prayed for a daughter, and Sati chose to be born again to them. She was Parvati, "she of the mountain", and tonight we call her Shailaputri, the daughter of the mountain. She is the same Devi, in a new family that honours the one she loves.

In pictures she rides a bull, which stands for steadiness. She holds a trident in one hand and a lotus in the other, and wears a crescent moon. A mountain doesn't move: tonight's form is about starting again from steady ground.

**[Pause: about 1 minute. Optional: "Which part of the story will you remember?"]**

## Part 2: The ideas and two Hogwarts scenes (about 12 minutes)

Three ideas come out of this story. People read them in different ways, so these are readings, not rules.

The first is standing by someone others scorn. Sati defended Shiva in her own father's house. Even when she was angry, she stopped Shiva's attendants from hurting her father. She loved both sides.

The second is that contempt costs the person who holds it. Daksha's pride cost him his daughter, his ceremony, and very nearly himself. He probably thought he was defending the proper way of doing things, and pride often believes it's being principled. And yet even Daksha got a second chance.

The third is beginning again from steady ground. Sati came back as the daughter of the mountain: the same Devi, and a new start.

**[Pause: ask, "Which of these three feels most true to you?"]**

Now, Hogwarts. This is from *Prisoner of Azkaban*, chapter two, "Aunt Marge's Big Mistake". It's near the start of the film too.

It's the summer Harry turns thirteen. Uncle Vernon's sister, Aunt Marge, comes to stay for a week with her bulldog, and Vernon makes Harry a deal. Behave yourself, never mention magic, pretend you go to a school for hopeless criminal boys, and I'll sign the form that lets you visit Hogsmeade with your friends.

So Harry bites his tongue. All week Marge sneers at him, and he takes it. Then, on the last night, after a long dinner, she starts on his parents. She talks about breeding dogs: if the mother's no good, the puppy won't be either. She calls his father a jobless good-for-nothing and repeats the lie that his parents died drunk in a car crash. Harry tells her they didn't. She carries on. Harry's anger rises past anything he can hold, and his magic bursts out of him. Marge swells up like a balloon and floats to the ceiling. Harry drags his trunk out of the house into the dark street, sure he'll be expelled. The form never gets signed.

**[PAUSE: Think it through. Choose two or three:]**

- **[Notice: what deal had Harry made, and what was he protecting by staying quiet?]**
- **[Feel: what was Harry feeling as Marge kept going? Where did it change?]**
- **[Wonder: why did Vernon and Petunia say nothing? Marge was insulting Petunia's own sister.]**
- **[Stretch: Harry didn't decide to inflate her. Is he still responsible?]**
- **[Connect: how is Harry at that table like Sati at her father's ceremony, and how is he completely different?]**

**[If they say "She deserved it", try: "Did she deserve it, or did Harry want it? Is that the same?" If they say "Sati and Harry both just got angry", try: "What did Sati do before she spoke?"]**

**[STANCE LINE: "Harry should have kept his temper: the deal mattered" or "Some insults have to be answered, whatever it costs". Each of you picks a spot and explains it in one sentence.]**

**[ACTIVITY: 3 minutes. Freeze the moment Marge insults Harry's father. Each of you has 60 seconds to suggest one thing Harry could say or do that defends his parents and keeps his control. Then judge: which protects his parents best, and which protects Harry best?]**

One thing to hold on to: Harry walking out of the house is *not* like Sati leaving her body. What's similar is the dilemma of sitting at a table while someone you love is mocked. What happens next is completely different.

Now a second scene, from *Philosopher's Stone*, chapter seven, with a moment from the end of *Chamber of Secrets*. Both are in the films.

It's Harry's first night at Hogwarts. One by one, the new students sit on a stool and put on an old, patched hat that decides their house. Harry already has an opinion. Hagrid told him that every witch or wizard who went bad came from Slytherin. And on the train he met Draco Malfoy, who was rude about Ron's family and certain he'd be in Slytherin.

The Hat speaks inside Harry's head. It sees courage, a good mind, talent and a hunger to prove himself, and it says Slytherin could help him become great. Harry thinks, as hard as he can: not Slytherin. After a pause, the Hat calls out: Gryffindor.

A year later, Harry is worried. He can talk to snakes, as Slytherin himself could. The Hat said he'd do well there; maybe he belongs there after all. Dumbledore points out that the Hat put him in Gryffindor because he *asked* not to go to Slytherin. It's our choices, Dumbledore says, that show who we really are, much more than our abilities.

Remember how Sati felt about being called "Daksha's daughter", and how she came back as the mountain's daughter? Both stories ask the same question: how much of who we are do we get to choose?

**[PAUSE: Think it through. Choose two or three:]**

- **[Notice: where did Harry's opinion of Slytherin come from?]**
- **[Wonder: was Harry choosing Gryffindor, or running away from Slytherin? Does it matter?]**
- **[Stretch: knowing about Snape, Regulus and Slughorn, was Harry fair to Slytherin?]**
- **[Turn it around: if the Hat had put him in Slytherin anyway, what about Harry would have stayed the same?]**
- **[Connect: what can we choose about who we are, and what can't we?]**

**[STANCE LINE: "Who you are is mostly what you choose" or "Who you are is mostly what you were given".]**

## Part 3: Side by side, today, and closing (about 7 minutes)

Let's put the two stories next to each other. There's no single right answer to any of these.

**[PAUSE: ask one or two, one at a time:]**

- **[Sati and Harry both sat through an elder insulting someone they loved. What was each trying to protect?]**
- **[Harry lost control; the story says Sati chose. Does it matter whether a response is chosen?]**
- **[After Harry's outburst, the Ministry quietly fixed Marge and changed her memory. After Shiva's fury, Shiva himself restored Daksha. Who repairs the damage in each story?]**
- **[What's one difference between these stories we should never forget?]**

Now, closer to home.

Imagine a big family gathering. An uncle makes fun of your older cousin's new partner, who comes from a different background. Everyone laughs, and your cousin goes quiet.

Some people would say speak up now, because silence looks like agreement. Some would say talk to the uncle privately later, because people dig in when they're challenged in public. Some would tell a parent and let an adult handle it. And some would let it go this time, because family peace matters too.

**[PAUSE: ask, "Which of these would you be proud of tomorrow? Which would your cousin want?" On the long path, also use the group-chat or new-school situation from Beat 15.]**

Last questions.

**[PAUSE: ask one question for your child, and one "go deeper" question if there's energy. Suggested: "Shiva warned Sati not to go. Why do you think she went anyway?" and "Can you be loyal to two people who are against each other?"]**

**[TOGETHER: "If someone in our family were mocked at a family gathering, what would we want each other to do?"]**

Tonight's story doesn't hand us an answer. It asks a question: when people close to us scorn someone we love, what do we protect, and how?

And it ends with a beginning. The daughter of the mountain is born. Tomorrow she sets out to reach Shiva, who is sitting in meditation and doesn't notice her at all.

**[End. Optional: each of you says, or writes in the app's private note, one thing you want to remember from tonight.]**
