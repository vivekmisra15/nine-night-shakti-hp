# Night 9: Siddhidatri, giver of perfection

The lesson and the read-aloud script for Night 9, the last night of the series. The plan for all nine nights is in `docs/NINE_NIGHTS_CONTENT.md`.

# Lesson

Night 9 takes about 30 minutes, or about 20 on the short path. Its question: what is the right way to hold power, and is the best way sometimes to put it down? The night tells of Siddhidatri, who gives perfection away, of Shiva who becomes half Devi, and of two seekers who ask the Devi for very different gifts. Then come two Harry Potter scenes: Harry laying down the Elder Wand, and the Mirror that gives the Stone only to someone who doesn't want to use it. The night closes the series with a look back across all nine nights, and ends with Vijayadashami.

## Beat 1: Before we begin

**Last night (recap):** Night 8 was about renewal after long effort. Parvati came out of her tapas shining as Mahagauri, and we followed Kreacher's change and Ron finding his way back to his friends.

**Tonight's form (tradition):** tonight is the ninth night, Mahanavami. The Devi is worshipped as Siddhidatri: *siddhi*, perfection or accomplishment, and *datri*, the one who gives. Tomorrow is Vijayadashami, the day of victory, which ends the festival.

**Contemporary:** if you have used the modern frame of the nine nights as nine steps of growth, tonight is the last step. Many people read it as completion: not having everything, but knowing what to do with what you have.

## The traditional story (Beats 2–8)

Siddhidatri has no long story of her own in the Puranas. What is told about her comes mainly from devotional tradition, and this beat says so. Around her we tell two older stories that fit her theme: Shiva as half-woman, from the Shiva Purana, and the gifts the Devi gives at the very end of the Devi Mahatmya (ch. 13). The Devi Mahatmya has been in the background since Night 3, so tonight we finish it.

### Beat 2: The giver of perfection (tradition, devotional)

In Navaratri worship, Siddhidatri sits on a full-blown lotus, or in some images rides a lion. She has four arms and holds a discus, a conch, a mace and a lotus. Around her, in many pictures, are gods, sages, celestial musicians, siddhas, and even asuras, the gods' old enemies. All of them come to her, because she gives *siddhis*.

A siddhi is a perfection, a power that comes from deep spiritual practice. The Bhagavata Purana (11.15.3–5) says there are eighteen, eight of them chief. Popular lists of the eight vary a little. They include becoming as small as an atom, becoming vast, becoming weightless, reaching anything anywhere, having any wish fulfilled, and mastery over nature.

**Hotspots (tap to learn), all labelled interpretation unless marked:**

| Symbol | Traditional meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Lotus seat | Purity and full flowering: the journey of nine nights has bloomed. (Tradition: the lotus seat; interpretation: its meaning.) |
| Discus (chakra) | Order and time turning; often read as the mind made steady. |
| Conch (shankha) | The first sound; a call that announces victory and drives away fear. |
| Mace (gada) | Strength, and the power to hold firm. |
| Lotus in her hand | Some traditions say the eight siddhis rest within it: power held gently, like a flower. |
| Worshippers of every kind | Gods and asuras alike come to her. Her gifts are not only for the "right" side, which raises the question of what people do with them. |

**Interpretation (yogic):** some devotional writers link the ninth night with the Sahasrara, the crown chakra, so the journey that began at the root on Night 1 ends at the crown. This is a modern devotional mapping, not a text, and accounts vary.

### Beat 3: Eight gifts (choice, hypothetical)

**Set-up:** imagine Siddhidatri offers you the eight siddhis. You could make yourself tiny or vast, weightless, reach anything, have any wish come true.

**Ask:** what would you do with them?

| Option | Acknowledgement shown |
| --- | --- |
| Take them all and use them | Honest. Most people would. The question is what you'd use them for. |
| Take one, and choose it carefully | Choosing one means knowing what you really need. That's harder than it sounds. |
| Take them, but promise never to use them | Holding power without using it is a real choice. Later tonight, Harry does something like it. |
| Say no, thank you | Turning down a gift from a Devi takes nerve. Some old teachers would agree with you. |

**What happens:** the traditions don't agree, and that's interesting. In devotional tradition, Shiva himself worshipped the Devi and received the siddhis (Beat 4). In the Bhagavata Purana, Krishna tells his friend Uddhava that wise devotees call these powers obstacles and a waste of time for anyone seeking the highest goal (BP 11.15.33). The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali say much the same: the powers are achievements to the outward-facing mind, and obstacles to the deepest meditation (Yoga Sutra 3.37; some editions number it 3.38). So the giver of powers is worshipped in a tradition that also warns about wanting them.

### Beat 4: Half of Shiva (tradition, two tellings)

**Devotional tradition (popular, not Puranic):** Shiva worshipped the supreme Devi and, through her grace, received the siddhis. By her grace too, half of his body became hers. He became Ardhanarishvara, "the lord who is half woman": one half Shiva, one half Shakti, in a single form. Many modern accounts say only that she is "believed to" have given him the siddhis. No Purana tells this story about Siddhidatri by name.

**Shiva Purana (Shatarudra Samhita, ch. 3):** an older story tells of Ardhanarishvara without naming Siddhidatri. Brahma, the creator, found that his creation would not grow. He did tapas, meditating on Shiva joined with Shakti, and Shiva appeared to him as Ardhanarishvara, half male and half female. Brahma asked the Devi for the power to bring the female half of creation into being, and asked her to be born as the daughter of his son, Daksha. She granted it. Then she merged back into Shiva's body.

So that is where our first night began. The Devi born as Daksha's daughter is Sati. Nine nights ago we met her in her father's house.

**Visual:** one figure, divided down the middle, whose two halves are one being. Calm, not strange.

### Beat 5: Two seekers by the river (tradition)

The whole Devi Mahatmya is a story told to two people. The first was King Suratha, who lost his kingdom to enemies; even his own ministers turned against him. He rode into the forest alone. There he met Samadhi, a merchant whose wife and sons had taken his wealth and thrown him out. The strange thing, they agreed, was that they both still worried about the people who had wronged them. They asked the sage Medhas why. In answer, Medhas told them the stories of the Devi: the stories of Katyayani, Kalaratri and the rest that we heard on earlier nights (Devi Mahatmya ch. 1–12).

When he finished, the king and the merchant went to a sandbank by a river. They made an image of the Devi out of earth. For three years they worshipped her with flowers, incense, fire and water, eating little and keeping their minds steady (DM 13.9–12). At last the Devi appeared and said: ask me for anything.

### Beat 6: What would you ask for? (choice, hypothetical)

**Set-up:** you are Suratha, or Samadhi. You have lost everything that mattered to you, through other people's greed and betrayal. The Devi offers you one gift.

**Ask:** what would you ask for?

| Option | Acknowledgement shown |
| --- | --- |
| My kingdom (or my wealth) back | It was taken unfairly. Asking for what's rightfully yours isn't greedy. That's close to Suratha's answer. |
| Make the people who betrayed me pay | Justice and revenge can feel the same from the inside. Would you be able to tell them apart? |
| To stop caring about what I lost | Freedom from the hurt itself. That's close to Samadhi's answer. |
| Something for someone else | Generous. Notice that the Devi asked what *you* wanted. |
| Nothing: seeing her is enough | Some devotional traditions honour exactly this answer. |

**What happens:** see Beat 7.

**Activity (3 minutes; this is the night's main activity):** one person argues for Suratha's kind of wish (get back what was taken) and the other for Samadhi's (let go of wanting it), 60 seconds each. Swap, and do it again. Then ask: which wish took more courage? Which would you actually have made?

### Beat 7: What the Devi gave (tradition)

Suratha asked for his own kingdom in this life, with his enemies' power broken, and a kingdom he could never lose in lives to come (DM 13.17). Samadhi had lost just as much. But he did not ask for his wealth. He asked for the knowledge that would free him from the feeling of "mine" and "I" (DM 13.18).

The Devi gave both of them what they asked. Within a few days Suratha would win back his kingdom and keep it. After this life he would be born as Savarni, a Manu, one of the great ancestors of humankind. Samadhi would receive the knowledge he asked for, which leads to freedom (DM 13.20–25). Then she vanished. And that is how the Devi Mahatmya ends.

**Notice:** the Devi did not judge either wish. One asked for power and one asked to be free of wanting, and both were answered.

### Beat 8: The tenth day (tradition and regional traditions)

Tomorrow is Vijayadashami: *vijaya*, victory, and *dashami*, the tenth day, in the bright half of the month of Ashvin. It celebrates victory, and different stories are remembered in different places.

- **Tradition:** the Devi's victory over Mahishasura (Devi Mahatmya ch. 2–3, Night 6). **Popular tradition** also places Rama's victory over Ravana on this day, and the day Arjuna took back the weapons the Pandavas had hidden in a shami tree during their exile.
- **North India (regional):** the Ramlila plays end, and huge effigies of Ravana and his brother and son, Kumbhakarna and Meghnad, are burned.
- **Bengal (regional):** Bijoya Dashami ends Durga Puja. Popular Bengali tradition says Durga visits her parents' home with her children each autumn and now returns to Shiva. Her images are carried to the river and immersed, married women smear each other with sindoor, and people exchange Bijoya greetings. (Compare Night 1: a daughter's visit home, this time with a welcome.)
- **Kerala (regional):** Vidyarambham. Small children write their first letters, often in rice or sand, to begin learning on this day.
- **Karnataka (regional):** Mysuru Dasara, with a great procession and the palace lit up. Families keep displays of dolls (gombe) through the festival.
- **Maharashtra (regional):** people exchange apta leaves as "gold".
- **Himachal Pradesh (regional):** Kullu Dussehra begins, with processions of deities from the valley's villages.

**Did you know? (regional tradition):** in much of South India, Ayudha Puja falls tonight, on the ninth day, not tomorrow. People honour the tools of their work: books, pens, musical instruments, machines, vehicles, and now laptops and phones. In many homes, books rest before the Devi overnight and nobody studies until they are taken up again on Vijayadashami.

## Beat 9: Three ideas (interpretation)

Each idea shows the simple version first, then the deeper one, then the story moments that support it.

**1. Power as a gift, held lightly**

- **Simply:** whatever you're good at, or in charge of, you were given at least partly. How you hold it matters.
- **From the story:** Siddhidatri gives perfection away; she doesn't keep it. Gods and asuras both come to her.
- **Traditional reading:** the Bhagavata (11.15.33) and the Yoga Sutras (3.37) both warn that special powers can become distractions from what really matters.
- **Other readings:** the Devi gave Suratha exactly the power he asked for, without blame. So wanting power is not wrong in itself. Perhaps the question is what it's for.

**2. Completion: two halves of one whole**

- **Simply:** being complete doesn't mean having everything. It can mean nothing is missing.
- **From the story:** Ardhanarishvara is Shiva and Shakti in one form. The Devi Mahatmya ends with two seekers both answered. The nine nights end where the first began, with the Devi who was born as Daksha's daughter.
- **Traditional reading:** Shiva and Shakti cannot be separated; consciousness and power belong together (Shaiva and Shakta interpretation).
- **Contemporary:** some people read Ardhanarishvara today as a picture of every person holding qualities called "masculine" and "feminine". That is a modern reading, not the traditional one.

**3. Honouring the tools of your work**

- **Simply:** your pen, your bat, your books and your instrument deserve respect, because they help you become who you are.
- **From the story:** Ayudha Puja tonight; Arjuna's weapons in the shami tree; children writing their first letters on Vijayadashami.
- **Traditional reading:** the power to act comes from the Devi, so the tools through which it flows are honoured.
- **Contemporary:** respecting a tool can also mean knowing its limits, and putting it down when it does harm.

## Beat 10: The Elder Wand (hogwarts)

**For the parent (show before this beat):**

- This scene comes right after the Battle of Hogwarts. Fred, Lupin and Tonks have died, and Harry has just walked into the forest expecting to die.
- The retelling doesn't dwell on any of this. If your child wants to talk about the deaths, give that space first. The rest of the night can wait.
- Harry's "death and return" is our comparison point only. It is not hidden Hindu theology, and the author did not intend a parallel with the Devi.

- **Reference:** Harry. *Deathly Hallows*, chapter 35, "King's Cross", and the end of chapter 36, "The Flaw in the Plan". Film: *Deathly Hallows Part 2* (2011), the closing scenes before the epilogue.
- **Find the scene:** there is no link. Find it yourselves in your own copy of the book or film, using the reference above.
- **Film note, and it matters tonight:** in the book, Harry uses the Elder Wand once, to mend his own broken wand, and decides to put it back in Dumbledore's tomb. In the film, standing on a bridge at Hogwarts with Ron and Hermione, he snaps the Elder Wand in two and throws the pieces away. The film does not show him mending his old wand *(to check)*. The film's King's Cross scene is much shorter, and Dumbledore's words about power come from the book *(to check that the film leaves them out)*. Keeping a power asleep and destroying it are two different ways of giving it up, and the difference is worth discussing.

**Retelling (spoken style).** Harry has walked into the forest and let Voldemort's curse hit him. He wakes in a bright, misty place that looks like King's Cross station, and Dumbledore is there. Dumbledore makes a confession. As a young man he hungered for power, and for the Deathly Hallows, and it led him into terrible mistakes. He was offered the job of Minister for Magic more than once, and refused, because he had learned he couldn't be trusted with power. Perhaps, he says, the people best suited to power are those who never went looking for it, like Harry.

Harry goes back, and the battle ends. Voldemort's own curse rebounds on him. The Elder Wand, the most powerful wand ever made, flies into Harry's hand. It belongs to him now, though he won it almost by accident, by disarming Draco Malfoy weeks earlier. Afterwards, in the headmaster's office, Harry tells Dumbledore's portrait his plans. He'll keep his father's Invisibility Cloak. He won't go looking for the Resurrection Stone, which he dropped in the forest. Then he lays his own wand on the desk, broken months ago at Godric's Hollow, and mends it with one touch of the Elder Wand. He'll put the Elder Wand back where it came from, in Dumbledore's tomb. If he dies a natural death, undefeated, its power will end with him. Ron thinks he's mad. Harry says the wand is more trouble than it's worth.

**The connection:** Idea 1, power held lightly, and Idea 3, honouring your own tool.

- **What is similar:** Harry is offered the greatest power in his world, and chooses to use it only once, to repair something small and his own. The traditions behind Siddhidatri also warn that powers can distract from what matters (BP 11.15.33). Dumbledore's confession sounds a little like Samadhi's wish: freedom from wanting.
- **What is different:**
    - Siddhidatri is the *source* of power and gives it away. Harry holds a power for a moment and lays it down.
    - The Elder Wand has a bloody history: in the story, people killed to own it. Siddhidatri's gifts are not won by defeating anyone.
    - The siddhis are understood in Hindu tradition as fruits of spiritual practice. Harry's wand is a magical object won in a war.
    - The Devi gave Suratha the power he wanted, without blame. Dumbledore suggests that wanting power is itself a warning sign. The two stories disagree here.
    - In the book Harry keeps the power asleep; in the film he destroys it.
- **Why the comparison helps:** it turns "the right relationship with power" into a choice a pre-teen can picture: what would you do if you were handed the strongest thing in the world?
- **Strength: strong.** The decision matches closely. The source of the power and its history do not.

## Beat 11: Think it through (comparison)

**The ladder**

1. **Notice:** what did Harry use the Elder Wand for, the one time he used it?
2. **Feel:** Ron was disappointed. How do you think Harry felt, holding it?
3. **Wonder:** why do you think Dumbledore told Harry about his own hunger for power, right then?
4. **Stretch:** in the book Harry keeps the wand's power asleep in a tomb; in the film he breaks it. Which is wiser, and why?
5. **Turn it around:** tell it from Ron's side. Is there a good argument for keeping the most powerful wand in the world?
6. **Connect:** Suratha asked for power and got it; Samadhi asked to be free of wanting. Which one is Harry more like here, and is he like either?

**If they say…, try asking…**

- "He should have kept it, he's the good guy" → "Would it stay safe with him forever? Who comes after Harry?"
- "Breaking it is better, then nobody can have it" → "Is destroying a power always better than leaving it unused? What about Siddhidatri, who keeps giving it?"
- "Harry didn't want power" → "He wanted to be an Auror and lead in the battle. What kind of power didn't he want?"
- "So wanting power is bad" → "Then why did the Devi give Suratha his kingdom without telling him off?"

**Stance line:** "The safest power is power nobody holds" ↔ "Power should be held by good people, not given up". Each person places a named marker, then explains it in one sentence.

**Activity: the wand on the desk (3 minutes, with a visible timer).** Freeze the moment the Elder Wand lies on the headmaster's desk. One person has 60 seconds to argue for the book's choice (put it back in the tomb), the other for the film's (break it). Then 60 seconds together: invent a third choice Harry didn't take, and decide whether it's better or worse than his.

## Beat 12: The Mirror and the Stone (hogwarts)

- **Reference:** Harry. *Philosopher's Stone*, chapter 12, "The Mirror of Erised", and chapter 17, "The Man with Two Faces". Film: *Philosopher's Stone* (2001), the Mirror scene at Christmas, the final chamber, and the hospital wing. The film keeps Dumbledore's explanation of the Mirror's rule.
- **Find the scenes:** there are no links. Find them yourselves in your own copies of the books or films, using the references above.

**Retelling (spoken style).** At Christmas in his first year, Harry finds an old mirror in an empty classroom. When he looks in it, he sees his mother and father behind him, and a whole family he has never met. He goes back night after night. Then Dumbledore finds him there. The Mirror, he explains, shows you the deepest wish of your heart. People have wasted their lives in front of it. It will be moved, and Harry shouldn't look for it again: it does no good to dwell on dreams and forget to live.

Months later, Harry goes down through the trapdoor and finds that same Mirror in the last chamber. Professor Quirrell is standing in front of it. Quirrell has learned from Voldemort that there is no good or evil, only power, and people too weak to go after it. He can see himself handing the Philosopher's Stone to his master, but he can't get it out. So he makes Harry look. Harry sees his reflection smile, put the Stone into its pocket, and wink. And Harry feels something heavy drop into his real pocket.

Afterwards, in the hospital wing, Dumbledore explains. He set the Mirror so that only someone who wanted to *find* the Stone, but not *use* it, could take it. Anyone who wanted gold or endless life would only see themselves using it. The Stone, he adds, has now been destroyed. Its maker, Nicolas Flamel, agreed, though without it he and his wife will one day die.

**The connection:** Idea 1, power as a gift, and Samadhi's wish.

- **What is similar:** the Mirror gives its treasure only to someone who isn't trying to gain from it. Samadhi asked not for his wealth but for freedom from wanting. And the Bhagavata warns that powers are a distraction for anyone who seeks them for themselves.
- **What is different:**
    - Harry didn't *choose* not to want the Stone. He was eleven and trying to stop a thief. Samadhi's wish came from long reflection and three years of worship.
    - The Mirror is a trap designed by a person. Siddhidatri gives freely, to gods and asuras alike.
    - In the Devi Mahatmya, Suratha *wants* power and receives it. In the Mirror's rule, wanting to use the Stone shuts you out. The stories disagree.
    - Quirrell's belief, that there is only power, is the opposite of both stories. Notice that neither story agrees with him.
- **Why the comparison helps:** it asks whether the people who should hold power are the ones who don't want it, and whether that's fair to people who do.
- **Strength: partial to strong.** The idea matches well; Harry's "choice" is more innocence than decision.

## Beat 13: Think it through (comparison)

**The ladder**

1. **Notice:** what did Quirrell see in the Mirror, and what did Harry see?
2. **Feel:** at Christmas Harry saw his family. How might it feel to be told to stop looking?
3. **Wonder:** Dumbledore says the Mirror shows your heart's desire. Why could Harry see himself *finding* the Stone, but not using it?
4. **Stretch:** Nicolas Flamel agreed to destroy the Stone, knowing he would die without it. Is that giving up power, or something else?
5. **Turn it around:** tell it from Quirrell's side. Is there anything true in "only power, and those too weak to seek it"?
6. **Connect:** Siddhidatri gives to everyone who comes to her. The Mirror gives only to those who don't want to use it. Which seems fairer?

**If they say…, try asking…**

- "Harry was just pure" → "Or was he just eleven? Would the Mirror still work for him at seventeen?"
- "People who want power shouldn't get it" → "What about someone who wants to be class captain to make things fairer?"
- "Quirrell is evil, so he's wrong" → "Can a bad person say something partly true? Which part?"
- "It's the same as Samadhi" → "Samadhi chose to let go after losing everything. Did Harry let go of anything?"

**Stance line:** "The people who should hold power are the ones who don't want it" ↔ "Wanting to lead is fine, if you know why you want it".

**Activity (skip on the short path): the Mirror test (2 minutes, with a visible timer).** Each person has 30 seconds to name something they'd love to have, and 30 seconds to say honestly whether they'd want to *find* it or to *use* it. Then swap. Could either of you get it out of the Mirror?

## Beat 14: Side by side (comparison)

Questions are revealed one at a time. None has a single right answer.

1. Siddhidatri gives power away; Harry gives the Elder Wand up. Is giving away the same as giving up?
2. The Devi gave Suratha the kingdom he wanted. The Mirror shuts out anyone who wants to use the Stone. Is wanting power wrong, or does it depend on why?
3. Book Harry keeps the wand's power asleep; film Harry breaks it. The Bhagavata calls the siddhis a distraction, yet Siddhidatri is still worshipped as their giver. What do you do with a power that is dangerous but also good?
4. Harry used the Elder Wand to mend his own old wand. Tonight, many families honour their own tools in Ayudha Puja. Why might the ordinary tool matter more than the powerful one?
5. Name one difference between these stories so big that we should never forget it.

**What doesn't compare (always shown):** Siddhidatri is worshipped as the giver of power itself, and her story is sacred. Harry is a fictional boy who won a wand almost by accident. We compare the choice about power, not the people.

## Beat 15: Today (contemporary)

There are three situations; the short path uses only the first. Each shows several perspectives a thoughtful person might hold, and none is marked as the right answer.

**1. The admin button.** You're an admin of your class group chat. One person keeps posting things that upset others. You could remove them with one tap.

- "Remove them. That's what admins are for."
- "Message them privately first. Everyone deserves a warning."
- "Ask the group. It's not my chat alone."
- "Hand admin to someone else. I don't want to be the one who decides."
- "Leave it. If I remove them, I become the bad guy."
- **Ask:** does having the button make it your job to use it?

**2. The answers in advance.** Your cousin did the same end-of-term quiz last year and offers you the answers. Nobody would ever know.

- "Take them. Everyone gets help from older siblings."
- "Say no quietly, and just revise properly."
- "Say no, and tell the teacher the quiz gets reused." But that could get your cousin in trouble.
- "Use them only to see what topics come up." Is that different?
- "Share them with the whole class, so it's fair." Is it?
- **Ask:** is an advantage unfair if nobody else can get it? What if everybody could?

**3. The captain who didn't want it.** The coach makes you team captain. You didn't ask for it. Your best friend wanted it badly.

- "Say no, and suggest your friend."
- "Accept, and share the big decisions with your friend."
- "Accept and lead your own way. The coach chose you for a reason."
- "Ask the coach to rotate the captaincy."
- **Ask:** Dumbledore thought the people best suited to power never sought it. Do you agree? Is that unfair to people who want to lead?

**Threads that kept coming back.** Pick two:

- **Choice versus what you were given (Nights 1 and 9):** Sati didn't want to be "Daksha's daughter". Harry chose his house, and later chose to put down the Elder Wand. Are you who your family made you, or who you choose to be?
- **The cost of protection (Nights 3, 5 and 7):** what does protecting someone cost the protector, and the protected?
- **Fierce and gentle together (Nights 3, 7 and 8):** which night showed this best to you?
- **Anger and justice (Nights 1 and 6):** when does anger become useful, and when does it destroy?
- **The right use of power (Nights 6 and 9):** who should hold power, and how?
- **Renewal and second chances (Nights 1 and 8):** Daksha was restored; Kreacher changed; Ron came back. Voldemort was offered the chance and refused it.

Take 60 seconds each to defend your picks. Then 60 seconds together: is there one night where you both changed your minds about something?

## For the parent

**What to remember**

- The heart of the night is a question, not a moral: what is power for, and when is putting it down the right choice?
- Be clear that the Siddhidatri story itself is devotional tradition, not Puranic. The Shiva Purana and Devi Mahatmya stories around it are textual, and the beats label which is which.
- Don't let "good people don't want power" become the lesson. The Devi gives Suratha his kingdom without blame. The stories disagree, and that is the point.
- Leave time for the look-back. It matters more than finishing every question tonight.

# Read-aloud script

Read it straight through, or use it alongside the app. It runs about 30 minutes with discussion, or about 20 on the short path. Lines in **[brackets]** are for the parent and are not read aloud. All Harry Potter lines are paraphrased.

## Part 1: Opening and the story (about 12 minutes)

**[Before you start: have nine diyas or candles ready for the end, or use the app's map.]**

Tonight is the ninth night, the last one. Last night was about renewal: Parvati shone again as Mahagauri after her long tapas, and we watched Kreacher change and Ron find his way back.

Tonight the Devi is Siddhidatri. *Siddhi* means perfection, or a power that comes from deep practice. *Datri* means the one who gives. So she is the giver of perfection. And tomorrow is Vijayadashami, the day of victory.

**[Pause: about 30 seconds]**

There isn't a long old story about Siddhidatri herself. What we know of her comes mostly from devotion: from how people picture her and pray to her. So tonight we'll hear what devotees say about her, and then two older stories that fit her well.

In pictures she sits on a lotus in full bloom. She has four arms, and holds a discus, a conch, a mace and a lotus. All around her are gods and sages, heavenly musicians, and even asuras, the gods' old enemies. They have all come for the same thing. She gives siddhis.

The old books say there are eighteen of these perfections, and eight that matter most. Becoming as small as an atom. Becoming vast. Becoming light as air. Reaching anything, anywhere. Having any wish come true.

**[PAUSE: Choice. Ask: "If she offered you all eight, what would you do? Take them all, take just one, take them and never use them, or say no thank you?" On the short path, just hear one answer.]**

Here's the interesting part. In one old text, the Bhagavata Purana, Krishna tells his friend that wise people call these powers obstacles, a waste of time for anyone seeking what really matters. The Yoga Sutras say something similar. So the giver of powers is worshipped in a tradition that also warns about wanting them.

Devotees say that even Shiva worshipped the Devi and received the siddhis from her. And through her grace, half of his body became hers. He became Ardhanarishvara, the lord who is half woman: half Shiva, half Shakti, one being.

The Shiva Purana tells a story about that form too. Long ago, Brahma the creator found his creation would not grow. He meditated on Shiva joined with Shakti, and Shiva appeared to him, half man and half woman. Brahma asked the Devi to help creation grow, and to be born as the daughter of his son, Daksha. She agreed, and then she went back into Shiva.

Daksha's daughter. Do you remember who she was?

**[Pause: let your child answer. It's Sati, from Night 1.]**

Yes. That's where our nine nights began.

Now the second story. It comes from the very end of the Devi Mahatmya, the text behind so many of our nights.

The Devi Mahatmya is really one long story told to two people. One was a king called Suratha. Enemies took his kingdom, and even his own ministers turned against him, so he rode alone into the forest. There he met a merchant called Samadhi, whose wife and sons had taken all his money and thrown him out. The strange thing, the two men agreed, was that they still worried about the very people who had wronged them. They asked a wise sage, Medhas, why.

To answer, Medhas told them all the stories of the Devi: of Katyayani and the buffalo-demon, of Kalaratri and the darkest battles. The stories we heard on earlier nights.

When he finished, the king and the merchant went down to a sandbank by a river. They shaped an image of the Devi out of earth. For three years they worshipped her there, with flowers, incense, fire and water, eating very little and keeping their minds steady. At last the Devi appeared. Ask me for anything, she said.

**[PAUSE: Choice. Ask: "You've lost everything through other people's greed. What would you ask for? Your kingdom back, revenge, to stop caring about what you lost, something for someone else, or nothing at all?" Listen to the reasons.]**

**[ACTIVITY: 3 minutes. One of you argues for getting back what was taken, the other for letting go of wanting it, 60 seconds each. Then swap. Ask: "Which wish took more courage? Which would you really have made?"]**

The king asked for his kingdom back in this life, with his enemies' power broken, and a kingdom he could never lose in lives to come.

The merchant had lost just as much. But he didn't ask for his money. He asked for the kind of knowledge that would free him from the feeling of "mine" and "I".

And the Devi gave both of them exactly what they asked. The king got his kingdom back within days, and in a future life he would become one of the great ancestors of humankind. The merchant received his knowledge, and with it, freedom. Then she vanished. And that is how the Devi Mahatmya ends.

Notice something. The Devi didn't scold either of them. One asked for power, one asked to be free of wanting, and she answered both.

**[Pause: about 30 seconds]**

Tomorrow is Vijayadashami. *Vijaya* means victory, and *dashami* means the tenth day. Different places remember different victories. The Devi's victory over Mahishasura. And in popular tradition, Rama's victory over Ravana, so across North India huge Ravana effigies are burned. In Bengal, people say Durga has been visiting her parents' home with her children, and now she goes back to Shiva. Her images are carried to the river. Think of Night 1: a daughter coming home, and this time, welcomed.

In Kerala, tiny children write their very first letters tomorrow. And tonight, in many South Indian homes, it's Ayudha Puja. People honour the tools of their work: books, pens, instruments, machines, even phones. Often the books rest before the Devi overnight, and nobody studies until tomorrow.

**[Pause: about 1 minute. Optional: "What tool of yours would you put in front of the Devi tonight?"]**

## Part 2: The ideas and two Hogwarts scenes (about 11 minutes)

Three ideas come out of tonight's stories. People read them in different ways, so these are readings, not rules.

The first is power as a gift, held lightly. Siddhidatri gives perfection away. The old books warn that powers can distract you. And yet the Devi gave the king the power he asked for, without blame. So maybe wanting power isn't wrong. Maybe the question is what it's for.

The second is completion. Shiva and Shakti in one form, two halves of one whole. The Devi Mahatmya ending with both seekers answered. Our nine nights ending where they began.

The third is honouring the tools of your work: the pen, the book, the instrument that help you become who you are.

**[Pause: ask, "Which of these three feels most true to you?"]**

**[For the parent: the next scene comes straight after the Battle of Hogwarts, when Fred, Lupin and Tonks have died and Harry has walked into the forest expecting to die. The retelling doesn't dwell on it. If your child wants to talk about those deaths, let them first.]**

Now, Hogwarts. This is from *Deathly Hallows*, chapter thirty-five, "King's Cross", and the very end of chapter thirty-six. The film tells it differently, and we'll come to that.

Harry has let Voldemort's curse hit him in the forest. He wakes in a bright, misty place like King's Cross station, and Dumbledore is there. Dumbledore makes a confession. When he was young, he hungered for power, and it led him into terrible mistakes. He was offered the job of Minister for Magic more than once and said no, because he'd learned he couldn't be trusted with power. Perhaps, he says, the people best suited to power are the ones who never went looking for it. People like Harry.

Harry goes back, and the battle ends. The Elder Wand, the most powerful wand ever made, flies into his hand. It's his now, though he won it almost by accident, by disarming Draco Malfoy weeks before.

Afterwards, in the headmaster's office, Harry tells Dumbledore's portrait what he'll do. He'll keep his father's Invisibility Cloak. He won't look for the Resurrection Stone he dropped in the forest. Then he lays his own wand on the desk, the one that broke at Godric's Hollow, and mends it with a single touch of the Elder Wand. He'll put the Elder Wand back in Dumbledore's tomb. If he dies a natural death, undefeated, its power will end with him. Ron thinks he's mad. Harry says it's more trouble than it's worth.

In the film it's different. Harry stands on a bridge with Ron and Hermione, snaps the Elder Wand in two, and throws the pieces away.

**[PAUSE: Think it through. Choose two or three:]**

- **[Notice: what did Harry use the Elder Wand for, the one time he used it?]**
- **[Wonder: why did Dumbledore tell Harry about his own hunger for power, right then?]**
- **[Stretch: book Harry keeps its power asleep; film Harry breaks it. Which is wiser?]**
- **[Turn it around: from Ron's side, is there a good argument for keeping it?]**
- **[Connect: the king asked for power, the merchant asked to be free of wanting. Is Harry like either?]**

**[If they say "He should have kept it, he's the good guy", try: "Who comes after Harry?" If they say "So wanting power is bad", try: "Then why did the Devi give the king his kingdom?"]**

**[STANCE LINE: "The safest power is power nobody holds" or "Power should be held by good people, not given up". Each of you picks a spot and explains it in one sentence.]**

**[ACTIVITY: 3 minutes. Freeze the moment the Elder Wand lies on the desk. One of you argues for the book's choice, one for the film's, 60 seconds each. Then 60 seconds together: invent a third choice Harry didn't take.]**

One thing to hold on to. Siddhidatri is the source of power, and she gives it away. Harry held a power for a moment and put it down. The Elder Wand was fought and killed over; the Devi's gifts aren't won from anyone. What's similar is the choice. Almost everything else is different.

Now a second scene, from *Philosopher's Stone*, chapters twelve and seventeen. Both moments are in the film.

At Christmas in his first year, Harry finds an old mirror in an empty classroom. When he looks into it, he sees his mother and father standing behind him, and a whole family he's never met. He goes back night after night. Then Dumbledore finds him there. The Mirror shows you the deepest wish of your heart, he explains. People have wasted their lives in front of it. It will be moved, and Harry shouldn't look for it again. It does no good to dwell on dreams and forget to live.

Months later, Harry goes through the trapdoor, and in the last chamber, there's the Mirror again. Professor Quirrell stands in front of it. Quirrell believes there's no good or evil, only power, and people too weak to go after it. He can see himself handing the Philosopher's Stone to his master, but he can't get it out. So he makes Harry look. Harry sees his reflection smile, put the Stone in its pocket, and wink. And he feels something heavy drop into his real pocket.

Afterwards, Dumbledore explains. He set the Mirror so that only someone who wanted to *find* the Stone, but not *use* it, could take it.

Remember the merchant, who didn't ask for his money back? Both stories wonder whether the person who doesn't grab for power is the one who should receive it. But they don't agree completely. The Devi gave the king exactly the power he wanted.

**[PAUSE: Think it through. Choose two or three:]**

- **[Notice: what did Quirrell see in the Mirror, and what did Harry see?]**
- **[Wonder: why could Harry see himself finding the Stone, but not using it?]**
- **[Stretch: Nicolas Flamel agreed to destroy the Stone, knowing he'd die without it. Is that giving up power, or something else?]**
- **[Turn it around: is there anything true in Quirrell's "only power" belief?]**
- **[Connect: Siddhidatri gives to everyone who comes. The Mirror gives only to those who don't want to use it. Which is fairer?]**

**[STANCE LINE: "The people who should hold power are the ones who don't want it" or "Wanting to lead is fine, if you know why you want it".]**

**[ACTIVITY (skip on the short path): 2 minutes. Each of you names something you'd love to have, then says honestly whether you'd want to find it or use it. Could either of you get it out of the Mirror?]**

## Part 3: Side by side, today, looking back, and closing (about 9 minutes)

Let's put the stories next to each other. There's no single right answer to any of these.

**[PAUSE: ask one or two, one at a time:]**

- **[Siddhidatri gives power away; Harry gives the Elder Wand up. Is giving away the same as giving up?]**
- **[The Devi gave the king his kingdom; the Mirror shuts out anyone who wants to use the Stone. Is wanting power wrong, or does it depend on why?]**
- **[Harry used the Elder Wand to mend his own old wand. Why might the ordinary tool matter more than the powerful one?]**
- **[What's one difference between these stories we should never forget?]**

Now, closer to home.

Imagine you're an admin of your class group chat. One person keeps posting things that upset everyone. You could remove them with one tap.

Some people would say remove them, because that's what admins are for. Some would message them privately first, because everyone deserves a warning. Some would ask the group. Some would hand admin to someone else, because they don't want to be the one who decides. And some would leave it, so they don't become the bad guy.

**[PAUSE: ask, "Does having the button make it your job to use it?" On the long path, also use the quiz-answers or team-captain situation from Beat 15.]**

And now, before we finish, let's look back at all nine nights.

**[Light one diya for each night as you read its line. Go slowly.]**

Night one, Shailaputri: what do you do when family scorns someone you love?

Night two, Brahmacharini: what is worth failing at many times?

Night three, Chandraghanta: how can someone be calm and fierce at once?

Night four, Kushmanda: can a small warmth begin a whole world?

Night five, Skandamata: how do you protect someone, and when do you let them go?

Night six, Katyayani: what happens when many powers join against injustice?

Night seven, Kalaratri: can something fearsome also be protective?

Night eight, Mahagauri: can anyone begin again?

Night nine, Siddhidatri: what is the right way to hold power?

**[Pause: let the nine flames burn for a moment in quiet.]**

**[PAUSE: ask one or two: "Which night stayed with you most?" "Which comparison was strongest, and which felt forced?" "Are you who your family made you, or who you choose to be?" On the long path, use the "defend a night" activity in Beat 16: 3 minutes.]**

Last questions.

**[PAUSE: ask one question for your child, and one "go deeper" question if there's energy. Suggested: "If Siddhidatri offered you one gift now, after nine nights, what would you ask for?" and "Suratha asked for power and Samadhi asked to be free of wanting. Is one wish better?"]**

**[TOGETHER: "What is one thing we want to carry from these nine nights into the rest of the year?"]**

Tonight's stories don't tell us to want nothing. The Devi gave the king his kingdom. But they keep asking the same question: what is our power for?

The giver of perfection gives it away. The boy who won the strongest wand in the world used it once, to mend his old one. And tomorrow is Vijayadashami, the day of victory, when the nine nights are complete.
