# Night 2: Brahmacharini, the one who practises

The lesson and the read-aloud script for Night 2. The plan for all nine nights is in `docs/NINE_NIGHTS_CONTENT.md`.

# Lesson

Night 2 takes about 30 minutes, or about 20 on the short path. Its question: when one way fails, what is worth practising for, again and again? The night tells how Parvati won Shiva not with charm but with tapas, and how her resolve was tested. Then come two Harry Potter scenes: the Patronus lessons that end at the lake, and, as a contrast, the Occlumency lessons that fall apart.

## At a glance

- **Intensity:** some peril. The story is gentle, though Kama is burned to ash. Read the parent note in Beat 6 (about fasting) before you begin. The second Harry Potter scene is *intense*, because it leads to Sirius's death: read the parent note in Beat 13 first. A gentle alternative is given there.
- **Short path (about 20 minutes):** ask two of the six ladder questions per scene, skip the second scene's activity (or use the gentle alternative), and use only the first "Today" situation.
- **Labels:** every beat carries one: *tradition*, *interpretation*, *contemporary*, *comparison*, *hogwarts* or *hypothetical*.

## Beat 1: Before we begin

**Last night:** Sati stood by Shiva in her father's house, even though it cost her everything. She was born again as Parvati, daughter of the mountain, in a family that honours the one she loves. But Shiva, deep in meditation, did not notice her at all.

**Tonight's form:** Brahmacharini, the second of the Navadurga in the Devi Kavacham. *Brahmacharini* is usually explained as "she who practises brahmacharya": a life of discipline, study and self-control, walked towards the highest truth (interpretation of the name). She is Parvati in the years of her tapas, the long, hard practice that finally reached Shiva.

## The traditional story (Beats 2–9)

This follows the Shiva Purana, Rudra Samhita, Parvati Khanda (chapters 11–28, in J. L. Shastri's English translation). Kalidasa tells the same story in his poem *Kumarasambhava*, cantos 3 and 5. That is a classical poem, not scripture, and the beats say so where it is used.

### Beat 2: The yogi who would not wake (tradition)

After Sati was gone, Shiva went to a high ridge of the Himalaya and sat in meditation, with his attendants keeping guard (ch. 11). Himavat, king of the mountains, came to honour him. He brought his young daughter Parvati and asked that she be allowed to serve Shiva. Shiva refused at first. An ascetic, he said, should keep away from such company, because it pulls the mind back into the world (ch. 12). Parvati answered him herself. Your very power to meditate comes from nature, she said, and nature is what I am. If you are truly beyond nature, why fear having me near? Shiva had no reply to that, and allowed her to serve him every day (ch. 13). So she came each day with flowers and water, and he went on meditating, as if she were not there.

### Beat 3: Kama's arrow (tradition)

The gods had their own reason to want Shiva married. A demon named Taraka had won a boon that only a son of Shiva could defeat him. (His story belongs to Night 5.) So Indra, king of the gods, called on Kama, the god of love, to turn Shiva's mind towards Parvati (ch. 17). Kama came with his wife Rati and with Spring. Flowers bloomed out of season and birds sang. As Parvati drew near with her flowers, Kama loosed his arrow. For a moment Shiva's meditation was disturbed and he gazed at her. Then he caught himself, ashamed of the lapse, and looked around for its cause (ch. 18). He saw Kama, bow in hand. Fire blazed from the third eye in Shiva's forehead, and Kama was burned to ash before the gods could cry for mercy (ch. 19).

Rati collapsed in grief. The gods begged Shiva to restore her husband. Shiva promised that Kama would live again, without a body for now, and later be born as Pradyumna, son of Krishna (ch. 19). Then Shiva vanished. Parvati, terrified by the blast, went home with her friends (ch. 21).

**Visual:** a single arrow of light, then a flash, then drifting ash and falling petals. No burning figure.

### Beat 4: What should Parvati do now? (choice, hypothetical)

**Set-up:** Parvati is heartbroken. She served Shiva for a long time, and the gods' plan used her beauty to reach him. It failed completely, and he has gone. The text says she even cursed her own beauty, because it had been useless (ch. 21).

**Ask:** If you were Parvati, what would you do now?

| Option | Acknowledgement shown |
| --- | --- |
| Give up: he has made his choice | Respecting someone's "no" matters. It's also how she might protect herself from more hurt. |
| Try again, but harder: more flowers, more beauty | Persistence is a strength. Notice, though, that this is the same approach that just failed. |
| Change the approach: become someone who can reach him | You're asking what *Shiva* values, not what usually works. That's a big shift. |
| Ask someone wise for advice | Knowing when you need a guide is its own kind of wisdom. |

**What happens:** some of the last two. The sage Narada came to her. Shiva left, he said, because he is a yogi whom charm cannot move. Reach him through tapas instead. He taught her the five-syllable mantra, *namah shivaya*, to repeat as she practised (ch. 21). Parvati decided to do it.

### Beat 5: "Oh, don't" (tradition)

Parvati asked her parents' permission to go to the forest for tapas. Her mother, Mena, was distressed. Practise at home, she said: all the gods and holy places are here. Your body is delicate, and this penance is severe. No woman has ever gone into the forest for a wish like this. Parvati did not give way, and at last Mena relented. The Shiva Purana says that because her mother forbade her, she came to be called Uma (ch. 22, vv. 18–27). In Kalidasa's poem the name comes from Mena's cry, "*u ma*": "oh, don't!" (*Kumarasambhava* canto 1, verse 26). That is a well-loved popular explanation of the name, not a scholar's one.

### Beat 6: Aparna, the one without leaves (tradition)

**For the parent (show before this beat):**

- Parvati's fasting is part of a sacred story about a Devi. The texts present it as extraordinary, not as something anyone should copy.
- Say plainly that growing bodies need regular food, and that skipping meals is not a way to show strength or love. Many adults fast during Navaratri; children are usually not expected to, and anyone fasting should do it with care.
- If your child has ever worried about food, eating or their body, keep this beat short and go straight on to Beat 7.

Parvati went to a mountain peak and began. In the first year she lived on fruit. In the second, on leaves. Then she gave up even leaves, and the gods called her Aparna, "the one without leaves" (ch. 22, vv. 47–49). She stood on one leg, repeating the mantra Narada had given her. In summer she sat in the middle of blazing fires. In the rains she sat on bare rock, soaked through. In winter she stood in cold water, day and night. Each day she watered the trees she had planted with her friends. Around her hermitage, animals that were natural enemies stopped fighting: lions and cows, cats and mice. Trees fruited and flowers opened, and the forest grew peaceful (ch. 22, vv. 40–45, 67–71). The text says she practised for three thousand years.

### Beat 7: Two tests (tradition)

Her tapas grew so strong that the gods felt its heat, and they went to Shiva. Shiva decided to test her resolve. First he sent the seven great sages (ch. 25). They told her that Narada misleads people. They described Shiva as homeless and ill-featured, with ghosts for company, a husband who had once left Sati. Go home, they said, and marry Vishnu instead; we will arrange it. Parvati laughed. She was born of a mountain, she said, and steadiness was in her nature. Even if the sun rose in the west, she would not change her mind. The sages blessed her and went back to Shiva.

Then Shiva came himself, in disguise: an old ascetic with matted hair, carrying an umbrella and a staff (ch. 26). Parvati welcomed him as an honoured guest. When her friend Vijaya explained what Parvati was practising for, he laughed (ch. 26). Then he set out to talk her out of it (ch. 27). Shiva smears himself with ash from the cremation ground, he said. He wears snakes and animal skins, and nobody knows his family. Remember what happened to Sati. Remember that he burned Kama and walked away from you. Why trade a jewel for a piece of glass?

### Beat 8: How should Parvati answer the stranger? (choice, hypothetical)

**Set-up:** a respected holy man, a guest she has honoured, tells her that everything she has suffered for is a mistake. Some of what he says is even true: Shiva did leave her.

**Ask:** If you were Parvati, how would you answer?

| Option | Acknowledgement shown |
| --- | --- |
| Argue back: he doesn't know Shiva | You trust what you've learned for yourself over what you've heard. |
| Listen: maybe he's right | Being willing to doubt yourself takes courage. Doubt can be a gift as well as a trap. |
| Walk away without a word | Some arguments aren't worth having. Silence can be its own answer. |
| Ask him why he cares so much | Sharp. Why would a stranger work so hard to change your mind? |

**What happens:** she argued, and then she walked away. You don't know Shiva, she told him (ch. 28). He is beyond every form, and takes a form only by his own will. His ash is worn even by the gods. She said that the person who listens to scorn of Shiva does wrong, not only the one who speaks it. She told Vijaya to stop the stranger talking, and turned to leave. As she turned, the ascetic took his own form. It was Shiva. He told her he had been testing her, and asked her to forgive the game. He said her tapas had won him completely, that he would never leave her again, and that she was his for ever. He asked her to come with him to his mountain. All the weariness of her tapas fell away (ch. 28, vv. 36–50).

**Activity (4 minutes; this is the night's main activity): the stranger at the door.** The parent plays the disguised stranger and has 60 seconds to talk the child out of a goal the child names (real or invented), using the stranger's tactics: some true points, some unfair ones. The child answers as themselves for 60 seconds. Then swap. Afterwards, ask: which of the stranger's points were fair? Which were tricks? How could you tell?

### Beat 9: The one who practises (tradition)

On this second night, Parvati is worshipped as Brahmacharini, the Devi in the years of her tapas. She walks barefoot, dressed in white. In her right hand she holds a rosary, and in her left a water pot. She is shown on foot: in most pictures she has no animal to ride.

**Hotspots (tap to learn), all labelled interpretation:**

| Symbol | Traditional meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Rosary (japa mala, right hand) | Repetition: one bead, one mantra, again and again. Practice made visible. |
| Water pot (kamandalu, left hand) | The ascetic's simple life: carry only what you need. |
| White clothes | Simplicity and purity of purpose. |
| Bare feet, and no mount | She walks her own path, step by step. Most Navadurga forms ride a lion, a bull or a tiger. |
| Calm face | Tapas is often called "heat", yet she is shown cool and peaceful. The fire is inside. In yogic interpretation, Night 2 is linked with the Svadhisthana chakra. |

**Did you know? (tradition and interpretation):** in Shastri's translation, Shiva's disguise is called a *brahmacharin*, a student ascetic (ch. 27). In Kalidasa's *Kumarasambhava* (canto 5) he also comes as a young brahmacharin. So the one who tested Parvati came dressed as the very thing she had become. And in both tellings, Shiva says her tapas had made him hers. (*Kumarasambhava* is a classical poem, not scripture.)

## Beat 10: Three ideas (interpretation)

Each idea shows the simple version first, then the deeper one, then the story moments that support it.

**1. When one way fails, change the way**

- **Simply:** if something doesn't work, trying the same thing harder isn't the only choice.
- **From the story:** charm, flowers and Kama's arrow all fail. Narada tells Parvati to try a completely different path, and she does.
- **Traditional reading:** Shiva cannot be reached by attraction, only by inner discipline. Tapas makes the seeker worthy of the one she seeks, which is why Shiva says her tapas has won him (ch. 28).
- **Other readings:** was it really Parvati's failure? The arrow was the gods' plan. The tapas was her own. Perhaps the story is about the moment she stopped being part of someone else's plan and made her own.

**2. Effort that lasts**

- **Simply:** some things can only come from practice, over a long time, when nobody is watching.
- **From the story:** fruit, then leaves, then nothing; summer fires, monsoon rock, winter water. Even the animals around her become peaceful.
- **Traditional reading:** *tapas* literally means heat. It is understood to burn away what is not needed and build spiritual power. Shiva himself had practised tapas for ages, and she meets him as an equal in it.
- **Other readings (contemporary):** today people talk a lot about "grit". But Mena's worry is in the story too. Is there a point where effort costs too much? Who gets to decide?

**3. Holding steady when tested**

- **Simply:** when someone tries to talk you out of what matters to you, can you stay steady and still listen?
- **From the story:** the sages and the stranger both use some true points. Parvati answers from what she knows of Shiva, not from what she wants.
- **Traditional reading:** devotion that can be talked away is not yet complete. Her answer shows that she knows Shiva, not just that she longs for him.
- **Other readings:** Mena doubted her too, out of love. Being steady is not the same as never listening. And Shiva asks forgiveness for the test. Is it fair to test someone you love?

## Beat 11: The Patronus lessons (hogwarts)

- **Reference:** Harry. *Prisoner of Azkaban*, chapter 12, "The Patronus"; chapter 13, "Gryffindor versus Ravenclaw"; chapter 20, "The Dementor's Kiss"; chapter 21, "Hermione's Secret". Film: *Prisoner of Azkaban* (2004).
- **Find the scenes:** there are no links. Find them yourselves in your own copies of the books or films, using the references above.
- **Film note:** the film squeezes weeks of lessons into one scene. Harry faints, tries a memory of flying, then succeeds with a memory of his parents. In the book, the lessons run through the term, his successful memory is a different one, and the progress is much slower. The book shows the effort better. The lake and the Time-Turner are close in both.

**Retelling (spoken style).** Dementors make Harry collapse, and he hates it. So after Christmas, Professor Lupin agrees to teach him the Patronus Charm. He warns Harry that it is very advanced magic. They use a boggart, which turns into a dementor in front of him. To cast the charm, Harry must hold a really happy memory in his mind. The first time, he hears his mother screaming and passes out. The second time, he hears his father's voice too, and passes out again. The third time, he chooses a stronger memory: the day he found out he was a wizard and could leave the Dursleys. Something silvery bursts from his wand and holds the dementor back for a moment. It isn't a full Patronus. The lessons go on, week after week. By the Quidditch match against Ravenclaw, he can drive off what he thinks are dementors.

Then, near the end of the year, a hundred real dementors gather at the lake around Sirius, Hermione and Harry. Harry's Patronus is too weak, and he falls. Across the lake someone casts a blazing Patronus that drives them away. Harry thinks it was his father. Later, Hermione's Time-Turner takes them back three hours. Harry waits on the far shore to see his father appear. Nobody comes. Then he understands: the person he saw was himself. He steps out and casts a full Patronus, a silver stag. Afterwards he tells Hermione he knew he could do it, because he had already seen himself do it.

**The connection:** Idea 2, effort that lasts, and Idea 1, changing the way.

- **What is similar:** repeated failure, practice that continues anyway, and success that comes from effort built over time. Like Parvati, Harry needs a guide who shows him the right method. Both reach the moment when nobody else is coming, and they have to do it themselves.
- **What is different:**
    - Harry wants a protective skill. Parvati's tapas is a spiritual discipline aimed at union with Shiva.
    - Harry has a kind, patient teacher and permission. Parvati went against her mother's wishes.
    - Harry practises for months; Parvati for "three thousand years". The scale is not the same.
    - The Time-Turner adds a magical twist: part of Harry's confidence comes from having seen the result. Parvati had no such proof.
    - Magic in Harry Potter is a learned and inherited skill. Tapas is understood as a sacred practice, not a technique.
- **Why the comparison helps:** most pre-teens know what it's like to fail at something three times in a row. This shows practice from the inside.
- **Strength: strong.** The pattern of failure, persistence and earned success matches closely.

## Beat 12: Think it through (comparison)

**The ladder**

1. **Notice:** what changed between Harry's first, second and third tries?
2. **Feel:** what might it feel like to hear your parents' voices every time you fail?
3. **Wonder:** why might part of Harry find it hard to stop hearing them?
4. **Stretch:** Harry succeeded at the lake partly because he'd already seen it happen. Does that make his success less his own?
5. **Turn it around:** tell the lessons from Lupin's side. What is it like to watch someone you care about faint, again and again, and keep going?
6. **Connect:** Parvati and Harry both wait for someone, Shiva or Harry's father. What changes when they stop waiting?

**If they say…, try asking…**

- "He just kept trying until it worked" → "Did he try the same thing each time? What did he change?"
- "It was only because of the Time-Turner" → "He'd practised for months first. Could he have done it at the lake without that?"
- "Parvati and Harry both worked hard" → "Was Harry's work the same kind of thing as Parvati's tapas? What's the difference?"
- "Lupin did all the work" → "Can a teacher do the practice for you? What can a teacher do?"

**Stance line:** "Harry succeeded because he practised" ↔ "Harry succeeded because he believed he could". Each person places a named marker, then explains it in one sentence.

**Activity: the third attempt (3 minutes, with a visible timer).** The child plays Lupin and the parent plays Harry, who has just fainted for the second time and wants to stop. Lupin has 60 seconds to persuade Harry to try once more, without just saying "don't give up". Then swap. Which arguments actually worked? Would any of them have worked on Parvati?

## Beat 13: Occlumency (hogwarts)

**For the parent (show before this beat):**

- This scene leads to Sirius's death in the Department of Mysteries. It is intense, and it can upset a child who loved Sirius.
- **Handle with care: the blame is not Harry's alone.** Voldemort set a deliberate trap. Snape taught with open contempt. Dumbledore kept his distance and kept the prophecy secret, and later says that this was his mistake. In the book, Kreacher lied that Sirius was not at home. Bellatrix cast the spell.
- Say it plainly: Sirius did not die because Harry didn't practise. The comparison is about practice and what makes it hard, not about whose fault a death was.
- **Gentle alternative:** if your child would rather not go there, use Hermione drilling Harry in the Summoning Charm before the first task (*Goblet of Fire*, chapter 20, "The First Task"). Harry thinks he has no strengths except flying, so he must learn to summon his broom. They practise through lunch and after dinner, and at about two in the morning it finally works. The next day his broom flies to him in the arena. The practice is in the book only; the film shows just the summons during the task.

- **Reference:** Harry. *Order of the Phoenix*, chapter 24, "Occlumency"; chapter 26, "Seen and Unforeseen"; chapter 28, "Snape's Worst Memory"; chapters 31–32, "O.W.L.s" and "Out of the Fire"; chapter 37, "The Lost Prophecy". Film: *Order of the Phoenix* (2007).
- **Find the scene:** there is no link. Find it yourselves in your own copy of the book or film, using the reference above.
- **Film note:** the film shows two lessons. Snape calls Harry weak and undisciplined, but the film doesn't show Harry skipping practice. The lessons end when Harry's shield charm turns Snape's spell back and Harry sees Snape's memory; in the book, he looks into Snape's Pensieve while Snape is out. The film also leaves out Kreacher's lie. So Harry's neglect of practice is mainly a book point; retell it from the book.

**Retelling (spoken style).** It's Harry's fifth year, and something strange is happening. He keeps dreaming of a long corridor ending in a closed door, and he sometimes feels what Voldemort feels. Dumbledore arranges for Harry to learn Occlumency: shutting his mind against intrusion. But Dumbledore won't explain why, and the teacher is Snape. In the lessons Snape forces his way into Harry's memories, sneering all the while. Between lessons, Harry is meant to clear his mind of every feeling each night before sleep. He hardly does. He resents Snape, and he doesn't really see the point. And part of him wants to see where the corridor leads. Then Harry looks into Snape's Pensieve and sees Snape being bullied by Harry's father. Snape is furious, and the lessons stop.

In the middle of an exam, Harry sees a vision: Voldemort torturing Sirius in the room behind that door. Hermione wonders whether it's a trick, and whether Voldemort is using Harry's urge to save people. Harry uses Umbridge's fire to check on Sirius at home, and Kreacher lies that his master is out. So Harry and his friends rush to the Ministry. It is a trap. The Order arrives to save them, and in the fight Sirius is killed. Afterwards Dumbledore tells Harry the whole story, and says much of the fault is his own.

**The connection:** this is a contrast. Idea 2, effort that lasts, and Idea 3, holding steady when tested.

- **What is similar:** a discipline that must be practised alone, every day, with no one watching. Both Harry and Parvati face a "test": Parvati a disguised stranger, Harry a false vision. Both tests use something they love.
- **What is different:**
    - Parvati chose her practice and knew exactly why she did it. Harry was given his practice and not told why.
    - Parvati's guide, Narada, was kind. Harry's teacher was hostile.
    - Harry was fifteen, grieving and angry. Parvati is a Devi.
    - The test of Parvati came from Shiva, who loved her. The test Harry faced was a trap set by his enemy.
    - Most important: Parvati's story ends in union. Harry's ends in a loss that many people, not Harry alone, helped to cause.
- **Why the comparison helps:** it shows the other side of practice. Discipline is hard when you don't trust the teacher, don't know the reason, or secretly want what you're supposed to shut out.
- **Strength: strong, as a contrast.** The question of daily practice matches closely; the outcomes are opposite.

## Beat 14: Think it through (comparison)

**The ladder**

1. **Notice:** what reasons did Harry have for not practising? List as many as you can.
2. **Feel:** how might Harry have felt, walking into Snape's office each week?
3. **Wonder:** why do you think Dumbledore didn't explain the reason for the lessons?
4. **Stretch:** many people made mistakes that led to the Ministry. Is it possible to share the blame without anyone being "the" cause?
5. **Turn it around:** tell it from Snape's side. He was asked to teach the son of the boy who bullied him.
6. **Connect:** Parvati knew why she practised; Harry didn't. How much does knowing *why* matter when practice is hard?

**If they say…, try asking…**

- "It's Harry's fault Sirius died" → "Who else made choices that led there? Who actually set the trap?"
- "Snape was a terrible teacher, so it's his fault" → "Is a bad teacher a reason not to practise? Or a reason it's harder?"
- "Harry should have just done what he was told" → "Would you, if nobody told you why and you didn't trust the person?"
- "Parvati would never have given up" → "Parvati chose her goal. Does it make a difference when someone else chooses it for you?"

**Stance line:** "Harry was responsible for practising, whatever Snape was like" ↔ "You can't expect someone to practise for a teacher who despises them".

**Activity: freeze at the doorway (3 minutes, with a visible timer; skip on the short path).** Freeze the moment before Harry leaves for the Ministry. Each person has 60 seconds to argue one side: "go now" or "stop and check another way". Then swap. Afterwards, ask: what would Harry have needed to know, or to have practised, to make a different choice?

## Beat 15: Side by side (comparison)

Questions are revealed one at a time. None has a single right answer.

1. Parvati, Harry at the lake and Harry in fifth year all practised something hard. What made the practice possible, or impossible, for each one?
2. Parvati changed her approach after failure. Harry changed his memory. Is changing the approach giving up, or getting smarter?
3. Parvati was tested by someone who loved her; Harry by someone who wanted to destroy him. Can a test ever be fair?
4. Mena worried about her daughter's tapas. Did anyone in Harry's story worry about him in the same way? What did they do?
5. Name one difference between these stories so big that we should never forget it.

**What doesn't compare (always shown):** Brahmacharini is a Devi, worshipped today, and her tapas is sacred practice. Harry is a fictional boy learning spells. We compare the struggle to keep practising, not the people.

## Beat 16: Today (contemporary)

There are three situations; the short path uses only the first. Each shows several perspectives a thoughtful person might hold, and none is marked as the right answer.

**1. Third time unlucky.** You've tried out for the school team twice and not made it. Trials are next month.

- "Try again, the same way. Third time lucky."
- "Ask the coach what you need to work on, and change how you practise."
- "Try a different sport. Maybe this one isn't for you."
- "Stop for now. It hurts each time, and that matters too."
- **Ask:** how do you tell the difference between giving up and choosing a better path?

**2. The teacher you can't stand.** A tutor sets you daily practice, but they're sarcastic and you think they don't like you. You've started skipping it.

- "Do the practice anyway. It's for me, not for them."
- "Tell a parent how the tutor makes you feel. It might be the teacher's problem."
- "Ask the tutor why this practice matters. Maybe knowing would help."
- "Find a different tutor, if that's possible."
- **Ask:** whose job is it to make practice worth doing: the teacher's, or the learner's?

**3. "You've changed."** You've been training hard for a big competition. You keep missing your friends' weekend plans, and one says you've changed and only care about winning.

- "They're testing whether I'm serious. Stay steady."
- "Maybe they've got a point. Is it costing me too much?"
- "Make time for one thing a week with them. Both can matter."
- "Real friends would support my goal."
- "Ask them what they miss. It might not be what you think."
- **Ask:** Parvati's mother and the sages both tried to stop her. When is someone's doubt a warning worth hearing?

## Beat 17: Reflection (contemporary)

Pick one or two questions; you don't need them all. The night's main activity, the stranger at the door, comes earlier, in Beat 8.

**For the child**

- Charm didn't work on Shiva. Why do you think tapas did?
- Which part of Parvati's tapas sounds hardest to you?
- In Harry's Patronus lessons, what kept him going after he fainted?
- What is one thing you'd be willing to fail at many times?

**Go deeper**

- Shiva tested Parvati, then asked her to forgive him for it. Is it ever right to test someone you love?
- Mena tried to stop Parvati out of love. Was she wrong?
- Harry wanted to see where the corridor led. Can you practise shutting out something you secretly want?
- Many people's choices led to the Ministry. How should we think about blame when it's shared?

**Together:** what is one thing each of us would like to practise this year, and how could we help each other keep going?

**Private note (saved only on this device):** one thing I'm going to keep practising is…

**Takeaway (shown last):** tonight's story asks what is worth practising, again and again, when one way has failed and others say stop. Parvati changed her path, held steady, and Shiva came to her. Tomorrow is the wedding: a procession so wild that Parvati's mother faints.

## For the parent

**What to remember**

- The heart of the night is a question, not a moral: what is worth failing at many times, and when should you change the way you try?
- Keep the fasting in Beat 6 clearly sacred and extraordinary, and say that children need food.
- In the Occlumency scene, say aloud that Sirius's death has many causes, and that Voldemort set the trap. Don't let "Harry didn't practise" become the lesson. Use the Summoning Charm alternative if needed.
- The story isn't only about "never give up". Parvati changes her approach, her mother's worry is loving, and Shiva apologises for his test. Point these out.

# Read-aloud script

Read it straight through, or use it alongside the app. It runs about 30 minutes with discussion, or about 20 on the short path. Lines in **[brackets]** are for the parent and are not read aloud. All Harry Potter lines are paraphrased.

## Part 1: Opening and the story (about 13 minutes)

**[Before you start: read the parent notes in Beat 6, about fasting, and Beat 13, about Sirius's death. Decide now whether to use the Occlumency scene or the gentle Summoning Charm alternative.]**

Last night, Sati stood up for Shiva in her father's house, and it cost her everything. She was born again as Parvati, the daughter of the mountain. But Shiva was sitting in deep meditation, and he didn't notice her at all.

Tonight the Devi is Brahmacharini, "the one who practises". This is the story of how Parvati reached Shiva. Not with beauty, and not with magic, but with years of practice.

**[Pause: about 1 minute. Optional: "What's something you've practised for a long time?"]**

After Sati was gone, Shiva went high into the Himalaya and sat in meditation. Himavat, the mountain king, came to honour him, and brought his daughter Parvati. Let her serve you, he said.

Shiva said no. An ascetic should keep away from company like that, he said; it pulls the mind back into the world.

Parvati answered him herself. Your power to meditate comes from nature, she said, and nature is what I am. If you're really beyond nature, why are you afraid of having me near?

Shiva had no answer. So she was allowed to serve him every day. She brought flowers and water, day after day. And he went on meditating as if she wasn't there.

Now, the gods had their own reasons for wanting Shiva married. A demon called Taraka could only be defeated by a son of Shiva. So Indra, king of the gods, called Kama, the god of love, and asked him to turn Shiva's mind towards Parvati.

Kama came with his wife Rati, and with Spring. Flowers bloomed out of season. Birds sang. As Parvati came near with her flowers, Kama let fly his arrow. For a moment, Shiva's meditation broke, and he looked at her.

Then he caught himself. He looked around for whatever had disturbed him, and he saw Kama, bow in hand. Fire blazed from the third eye in Shiva's forehead, and Kama was burned to ash.

Rati fell to the ground, weeping. The gods begged for mercy, and Shiva promised that Kama would live again one day. Then Shiva vanished. Parvati, terrified by the blast, ran home.

**[PAUSE: Choice. Ask: "Parvati is heartbroken. The plan to win Shiva with beauty has failed, and he's gone. What would you do now? Give up, try harder, change your whole approach, or ask someone wise?" Listen to the reasons.]**

What happened was the last two. The wise sage Narada came to her. Shiva is a yogi, he said. Charm won't move him. Reach him through tapas: through long, hard spiritual practice. And he taught her a mantra to repeat, *namah shivaya*.

Parvati asked her parents if she could go to the forest to do it. Her mother, Mena, was frightened for her. Practise here at home, she said. Your body is delicate. Nobody does this. One poet tells it that Mena cried out "*u ma*", "oh, don't!", and that's how Parvati got her other name, Uma. But Parvati wouldn't change her mind, and at last her mother let her go.

**[For the parent: before the next part, say in your own words that this is a sacred story about a Devi, not something anyone should copy, and that growing bodies need regular food. If food or body worries are a sensitive subject for your child, keep the next paragraph short.]**

So Parvati went up a mountain and began. In the first year she ate only fruit. In the second, only leaves. Then she gave up even leaves, and the gods called her Aparna, "the one without leaves". She stood on one leg, repeating her mantra. In summer she sat among blazing fires. In the monsoon she sat on bare rock in the pouring rain. In winter she stood in icy water. And every day she watered the trees she'd planted.

Something strange happened around her. Animals that were natural enemies stopped fighting. Lions and cows, cats and mice. The whole forest grew peaceful. The story says she practised for three thousand years.

Her tapas grew so strong that the gods felt its heat. Shiva decided to test her.

First he sent seven great sages. Narada has fooled you, they told her. Shiva is homeless and strange, and he once left Sati. Marry Vishnu instead; we'll arrange it. Parvati laughed. I was born of a mountain, she said. Even if the sun rose in the west, I wouldn't change my mind.

Then Shiva came himself, in disguise, as an old holy man with matted hair, an umbrella and a staff. Parvati welcomed him as an honoured guest. When he heard what she was practising for, he laughed. Shiva smears himself with ash from funeral fires, he said. He wears snakes. Nobody knows his family. Remember what happened to Sati. Remember he burned Kama and walked away from you. Why trade a jewel for a piece of glass?

**[PAUSE: Choice. Ask: "If you were Parvati, how would you answer? Argue back, listen, walk away, or ask him why he cares so much?"]**

**[ACTIVITY: 4 minutes. You play the stranger. For 60 seconds, try to talk your child out of a goal they name (real or invented), using some fair points and some unfair ones. They answer for 60 seconds. Then swap. Ask: "Which points were fair, and which were tricks? How could you tell?"]**

Parvati argued, and then she walked away. You don't know Shiva, she told him. He is beyond every shape, and takes a shape only when he chooses. And whoever listens to scorn like yours does wrong, not just the one who speaks it. She told her friend to stop him talking, and she turned to go.

As she turned, the old man changed. It was Shiva. I was testing you, he said, and asked her to forgive him for it. Your tapas has won me completely. I will never leave you again. Come with me to my mountain. And all the tiredness of those years fell away from her.

On this second night, we call her Brahmacharini, the one who practises. She walks barefoot, dressed in white. In one hand she holds a string of prayer beads, for repeating her mantra again and again. In the other she holds a water pot, because she carries only what she needs. Most of the Devi's forms ride a lion or a bull. She walks.

**[Pause: about 1 minute. Optional: "Which part of the story will you remember?"]**

## Part 2: The ideas and two Hogwarts scenes (about 11 minutes)

Three ideas come out of this story. People read them in different ways, so these are readings, not rules.

The first is: when one way fails, change the way. Beauty and Kama's arrow failed. Parvati didn't just try harder; she tried something completely different. And the arrow was the gods' plan. The tapas was hers.

The second is effort that lasts. Tapas means heat: practice so steady that it changes you. But her mother's worry is in the story too. Can effort ever cost too much?

The third is holding steady when you're tested. The sages and the stranger said some things that were true. Parvati answered from what she knew. And yet Shiva asked her forgiveness for testing her. Is it fair to test someone you love?

**[Pause: ask, "Which of these three feels most true to you?"]**

Now, Hogwarts. This is from *Prisoner of Azkaban*, chapter twelve, "The Patronus", and the end of the book, chapters twenty and twenty-one. It's in the film too, though the film squeezes the lessons into one scene.

Dementors make Harry collapse, and he hates it. So Professor Lupin agrees to teach him the Patronus Charm, and warns him that it's very advanced magic. They use a boggart, which turns into a dementor. Harry has to fill his mind with a really happy memory.

The first time, he hears his mother screaming, and faints. The second time, he hears his father's voice too, and faints again. The third time he picks a stronger memory: the day he found out he was a wizard and could leave the Dursleys. Something silvery bursts from his wand and holds the dementor back, just for a moment. Week after week, the lessons go on.

Then, at the end of the year, a hundred dementors close in at the lake. Harry's Patronus is too weak, and he falls. Someone across the lake casts a huge Patronus and drives them away. Harry thinks it was his father.

Later, Hermione's Time-Turner takes them back. Harry waits on the far shore to see his father appear. Nobody comes. And then he understands. The person he saw was himself. He steps out and casts a full Patronus: a silver stag. Afterwards he tells Hermione he knew he could do it, because he'd already seen himself do it.

**[PAUSE: Think it through. Choose two or three:]**

- **[Notice: what changed between Harry's first, second and third tries?]**
- **[Feel: what might it feel like to hear your parents' voices every time you fail?]**
- **[Stretch: Harry had already seen himself succeed. Does that make it less his own?]**
- **[Turn it around: tell the lessons from Lupin's side.]**
- **[Connect: Parvati and Harry both wait for someone. What changes when they stop waiting?]**

**[If they say "He just kept trying", try: "Did he try the same thing each time?" If they say "Lupin did all the work", try: "Can a teacher do the practice for you?"]**

**[STANCE LINE: "Harry succeeded because he practised" or "Harry succeeded because he believed he could". Each of you picks a spot and explains it in one sentence.]**

**[ACTIVITY: 3 minutes. Your child plays Lupin; you play Harry, who has just fainted twice and wants to stop. Lupin has 60 seconds to persuade Harry to try again, without just saying "don't give up". Then swap.]**

One thing to hold on to: Harry is learning a spell, and Parvati's tapas is a sacred practice. What's similar is failing, and keeping going, and changing what you try.

**[For the parent: the next scene leads to Sirius's death. If you're using the gentle alternative instead, retell Hermione drilling Harry in the Summoning Charm until two in the morning before the first task (*Goblet of Fire*, chapter 20), then skip to the stance line below. If you use the Occlumency scene, say plainly afterwards that Sirius did not die because Harry didn't practise: Voldemort set a trap, and many people's choices led there.]**

Now a second scene, and it's a contrast. It's from *Order of the Phoenix*, chapters twenty-four to thirty-seven. The film shows less of it.

In his fifth year, Harry keeps dreaming of a long corridor ending in a closed door. Sometimes he can feel what Voldemort feels. Dumbledore arranges for him to learn Occlumency, which means shutting his mind against someone breaking in. But Dumbledore won't say why, and the teacher is Snape.

Snape forces his way into Harry's memories, sneering the whole time. Between lessons, Harry is supposed to clear his mind every night before he sleeps. He hardly ever does. He can't stand Snape, and he doesn't see the point. And part of him wants to know what's behind that door.

Then Harry looks into Snape's Pensieve, and sees Snape being bullied by Harry's own father. Snape is furious, and the lessons stop.

In the middle of an exam, Harry has a vision of Voldemort torturing Sirius. Hermione wonders if it's a trick. Harry checks through Umbridge's fire, and Kreacher lies that Sirius is out. So Harry and his friends rush to the Ministry. It's a trap. The Order comes to rescue them, and in the fight, Sirius is killed. Afterwards, Dumbledore tells Harry everything, and says much of the fault is his.

**[Pause: give a moment of quiet. Ask: "How are you feeling about that?" Then say plainly: "Sirius didn't die because Harry didn't practise. Voldemort set the trap, and a lot of people made mistakes."]**

Parvati knew exactly why she practised, and she chose it. Harry was given his practice, wasn't told why, and had a teacher who despised him. Both of them were tested. Parvati's test came from someone who loved her. Harry's came from his enemy.

**[PAUSE: Think it through. Choose two or three:]**

- **[Notice: what reasons did Harry have for not practising?]**
- **[Wonder: why didn't Dumbledore explain?]**
- **[Stretch: lots of people made mistakes. Can blame be shared without anyone being "the" cause?]**
- **[Turn it around: Snape had to teach the son of the boy who bullied him. Tell it from his side.]**
- **[Connect: how much does knowing *why* matter when practice is hard?]**

**[If they say "It's Harry's fault Sirius died", try: "Who else made choices that led there? Who set the trap?"]**

**[STANCE LINE: "Harry was responsible for practising, whatever Snape was like" or "You can't expect someone to practise for a teacher who despises them". With the gentle alternative, use: "Hermione's drilling made the difference" or "Harry's own effort made the difference".]**

**[ACTIVITY (skip on the short path): 3 minutes. Freeze the moment before Harry leaves for the Ministry. One of you argues "go now", the other "stop and check another way", 60 seconds each. Then swap.]**

## Part 3: Side by side, today, and closing (about 7 minutes)

Let's put the stories next to each other. There's no single right answer to any of these.

**[PAUSE: ask one or two, one at a time:]**

- **[What made practice possible, or impossible, for Parvati and for Harry?]**
- **[Parvati changed her approach after failing. Is changing the approach giving up, or getting smarter?]**
- **[Parvati was tested by someone who loved her; Harry by his enemy. Can a test ever be fair?]**
- **[What's one difference between these stories we should never forget?]**

Now, closer to home.

Imagine you've tried out for the school team twice and not made it. Trials are next month.

Some people would say try again, the same way: third time lucky. Some would ask the coach what to work on, and change how they practise. Some would try a different sport. And some would stop for now, because it hurts every time, and that matters too.

**[PAUSE: ask, "How do you tell the difference between giving up and choosing a better path?" On the long path, also use the tutor or "You've changed" situation from Beat 16.]**

Last questions.

**[PAUSE: ask one question for your child, and one "go deeper" question if there's energy. Suggested: "Charm didn't work on Shiva. Why do you think tapas did?" and "Shiva tested Parvati, then asked her to forgive him. Is it ever right to test someone you love?"]**

**[TOGETHER: "What's one thing each of us would like to practise this year, and how could we help each other keep going?"]**

Tonight's story asks what's worth practising, again and again, when one way has failed and other people say stop. Parvati changed her path, held steady, and Shiva came to her.

Tomorrow is the wedding. Shiva arrives with a procession so wild that Parvati's mother faints.

**[End. Optional: each of you says, or writes in the app's private note, one thing you're going to keep practising.]**
